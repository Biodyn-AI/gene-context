\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{Background:} Single-cell foundation models read a cell as a
sequence of its genes and build, at every layer, a representation of
each gene that depends on the other genes in the cell. Whether this
contextual representation carries information about a gene's role in a
given cell type, the single-cell counterpart of word sense in language
models, has not been tested.

\textbf{Results:} We measured contextual gene representations in MaxToki
(two sizes), scGPT and STATE, in 12 Tabula Sapiens cell types and an
independent bone-marrow dataset. A gene's representation changes with
cell type in a gene-specific way: the same gene's change agrees across
independent cells at cosine 0.76, against 0.001 for a gene-shuffled
control. The effect is strongest in the first two layers, survives rank
control (99\%), replicates on the independent dataset (0.71), is partly
learned (untrained model: 0.44 against 0.74 on the same cells) and does
not grow consistently with model size. Genes that change more gain
somewhat more from real context in next-gene prediction (partial
correlation 0.16). The changes follow functional axes such as nuclear
versus surface genes (z = 17 against random axes), but gene sets matched
on size and co-expression give similar organisation (p = 0.08--0.38).
Steering along the nuclear/surface axis, with large pushes, shifts the
model's predictions for other genes (p = 0.0081); a second axis has no
consistent effect and a third acts in reverse. Two tests for
context-specific gene function, run in MaxToki-217M, were negative:
transcription factors sit slightly closer to their curated targets than
co-expression predicts, but not significantly more so where those
targets are active. Functional organisation differs sharply between
models (z = 8.1--18.4 for MaxToki, 3.2 for scGPT, 1.1 for STATE).

\textbf{Conclusions:} Single-cell foundation models build real
contextual gene representations. In MaxToki, training organises them
along functional axes no more than co-expression does, and we found no
context-specific gene function beyond co-expression. Reading a gene's
changed role in a new cell type will likely need models trained on more
than expression. The functional organisation of contextual change offers
a simple metric for comparing architectures.

\textbf{Keywords:} Single-cell foundation models; Contextual gene
representations; Mechanistic interpretability; Gene co-expression; Gene
embeddings; Transformers; Single-cell RNA sequencing; Model evaluation

\section{Background}\label{background}

A recurring theme in the interpretability of large language models is
that a network trained on a narrow predictive task builds internal
representations of structure it was never taught. Sequence models of
Othello and chess build representations of the board and of latent
variables such as player skill (Li et al. 2023; Nanda et al. 2023;
Karvonen 2024); language models linearly encode space and time along
identifiable directions (Gurnee and Tegmark 2024); and, in the best
cases, these representations are not decorative but causally used, a
claim one tests by intervening on the representation and measuring the
effect on behaviour (Turner et al. 2023; {Zou et al.} 2023; Park et al.
2024).

A closely related line concerns contextual representations. Contextual
word embeddings assign the same word a different vector in every
sentence, and the geometry of those vectors encodes word sense: ``bank''
near a river and ``bank'' in finance occupy separable regions (Peters et
al. 2018; Ethayarajh 2019; Coenen et al. 2019). These measurements need
care, because representation spaces are strongly anisotropic (vectors
cluster in a narrow cone rather than spreading evenly, so any two look
similar) and a handful of ``rogue'' dimensions can dominate cosine
similarity (Ethayarajh 2019; Mu et al. 2018; Cai et al. 2021; {Timkey
and van Schijndel} 2021).

Single-cell foundation models (SCFMs) offer a clean biological version
of the same question (Theodoris et al. 2023; Cui et al. 2024; Hao et al.
2024; Yang et al. 2022). They treat each gene as a token and each cell
as a set or sequence of genes, either ordered by expression (Geneformer)
or paired with binned or continuous expression values (scGPT, scBERT,
scFoundation), and are trained to predict masked or next genes or
values, in effect learning which genes tend to be active together. Their
gene token embeddings (one fixed vector per gene) have been studied (Cui
et al. 2024; Kendiukhov 2026g); their contextual gene representations (a
gene's vector inside a particular cell) are used as features downstream
(Hao et al. 2024; Bian et al. 2024) but have not been characterised as
objects in their own right. Yet this is exactly where a model could, in
principle, represent that a gene plays a different role in a macrophage
than in a T cell: the biological analogue of word sense, and an open
direction that the ``cell as a token'' framing invites but does not test
(Gilpin 2025).

\textbf{Related work.} Our methods descend directly from the
language-model literature on contextualised embeddings. Ethayarajh
(Ethayarajh 2019) showed that contextual representations are anisotropic
in all non-input layers, that same-word self-similarity falls in upper
layers, and that every cosine must be corrected against an anisotropy
baseline. Timkey and van Schijndel ({Timkey and van Schijndel} 2021)
showed that a few rogue dimensions dominate similarity and that a scalar
correction is not enough; Mu et al. (Mu et al. 2018) and Cai et al. (Cai
et al. 2021) give the standard postprocessing and the local-isotropy
picture. Coenen et al. (Coenen et al. 2019) and Garí Soler and
Apidianaki (Garí Soler and Apidianaki 2021) show that word senses form
separable clusters and that a word's degree of polysemy is itself
readable. We import both the measurements and the controls. From the
literature on emergent, causally used latent variables (Li et al. 2023;
Nanda et al. 2023; Karvonen 2024; Gurnee and Tegmark 2024) we adopt the
evidentiary standard that a probe shows only correlation, so a causal
claim needs an intervention against a matched control, together with its
steering methods (Turner et al. 2023; {Zou et al.} 2023), of which the
extraction and amplification of interpretable feature directions in
production models is the large-scale instance ({Templeton et al.} 2024).

Geneformer (Theodoris et al. 2023), scGPT (Cui et al. 2024),
scFoundation (Hao et al. 2024), scBERT (Yang et al. 2022), UCE (Rosen et
al. 2026) and STATE ({Adduri et al.} 2026) differ in tokenisation
(rank-ordered gene identity, binned or continuous expression values, or
gene tokens derived from protein-sequence embeddings), and the review of
Bian et al. (Bian et al. 2024) defines the ``contextual gene embedding''
that our work dissects. MaxToki ({Gómez Ortega et al.} 2026), the
primary model here, is a Llama-architecture, Geneformer-lineage model at
217M and 1B scales. Two earlier analyses come closest to a ``context
changes the gene'' claim. scGPT reports cell-state-specific gene
networks read from attention (Cui et al. 2024), but as a pairwise
network, not as a property of the representation vector, and without a
decomposition or a null. Geneformer's in silico deletion measures how
the embeddings of the other genes shift when one gene is removed from a
cell (Theodoris et al. 2023), which is a change of context by one gene.
Neither characterises how a gene's representation differs between
natural cell types. The geometry of gene representations in these models
has been mapped, both for static gene embeddings (Kendiukhov 2026g) and
for layer-wise hidden states averaged over cells, including their
spectral structure (Kendiukhov 2026c) and the topological and geometric
structure they do and do not encode, per tissue (Kendiukhov 2026h).
Residual-stream gene vectors built separately in several tissues carry a
small, tissue-dependent amount of transcription-factor-target signal
beyond expression covariates (Kendiukhov 2026d). How much, and how
gene-specifically, a single gene's vector changes from one cell type to
another, the object here, has not been measured.

A critical literature finds that SCFMs often fail to beat simple
baselines: on zero-shot tasks (Kedzierska et al. 2025), under deeper
evaluation (Boiarsky et al. 2024), on perturbation prediction
(Ahlmann-Eltze et al. 2025; Bendidi et al. 2024), and across utilities
(Liu et al. 2026). Sparse autoencoders find organised biological
knowledge but minimal regulatory logic in these models (Kendiukhov
2026e), and edge scores read from SCFM attention add nothing beyond
simple gene-level statistics for predicting which genes respond to a
perturbation (Kendiukhov 2026f). Co-expression is the canonical
guilt-by-association signal ({van Dam et al.} 2018), and some
gene-embedding methods, such as Gene2vec, are built directly on it (Du
et al. 2019); others build gene embeddings from text descriptions of
genes instead (Chen and Zou 2025). Genuine context-dependent gene
function exists. Some of it acts at the protein level, as in
moonlighting proteins (Jeffery 1999), and leaves no expression
signature. Some acts through transcription, as when lineage-determining
transcription factors select different enhancers with different partners
by cell type (Heinz et al. 2010).

\textbf{Aim.} We organise the question into two levels. Level 1 (a fact
about the model): is a gene's representation reshaped by context in a
structured, gene-specific way, and is that reshaping learned and used?
Level 2 (biology): does the reshaping reveal something about the gene's
role in each context beyond what co-expression already tells us? We
answer Level 1 for MaxToki-217M with a rank control, an independent
replication, a random-weights control, a link to the model's predictions
and a steering test, and compare contextualisation strength and
functional organisation across four models. For Level 2, we test the
functional organisation of the contextualisation against co-expression
nulls and run two direct tests for context-specific gene function, all
in MaxToki-217M. The study makes three contributions: (1) the Level 1
characterisation; (2) the Level 2 boundary; and (3) contextualisation as
a model-characterisation metric.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Models}\label{models}

MaxToki-217M and MaxToki-1B ({Gómez Ortega et al.} 2026; Theodoris Lab
2026) are Llama-architecture autoregressive models over rank-ordered
gene tokens, with 11 and 20 transformer layers and hidden widths of 1232
and 2304. We used the Hugging Face safetensors checkpoints through the
\texttt{transformers} library with \texttt{output\_hidden\_states=True},
in float32. For MaxToki, ``layer \emph{k}'' is the \emph{k}-th hidden
state: layer 0 is the embedding output and layer \emph{k} ≥ 1 is the
output of the \emph{k}-th transformer block; the last hidden state
(layer 11 of MaxToki-217M) is taken after the model's final
normalisation. scGPT (Cui et al. 2024; Bo Wang Lab, n.d.) (whole-human
checkpoint; 12 layers; width 512) and STATE ({Adduri et al.} 2026; Arc
Institute 2025) (the SE-600M state-embedding model; 16 layers; width
2048) were used for the cross-architecture comparison. STATE's gene
tokens are fixed protein embeddings from the Evolutionary Scale Modeling
2 (ESM2) language model ({Lin et al.} 2023), passed through a learned
projection together with each gene's expression. For scGPT and STATE,
``layer \emph{k}'' is the output of the transformer module with index
\emph{k} (the (\emph{k}+1)-th block). As an untrained control, we built
a \texttt{LlamaForCausalLM} from MaxToki-217M's configuration with
random initial weights (the library's default initialisation, seed 0)
and the same vocabulary.

\subsection{Datasets and annotations}\label{datasets-and-annotations}

The main data are the Tabula Sapiens (Tabula Sapiens Consortium 2022)
immune, kidney and lung datasets from CZ CELLxGENE Discover ({CZI Cell
Science Program et al.} 2025; Tabula Sapiens Consortium, n.d.-a, n.d.-b,
n.d.-c). For the immune dataset we used a 20,000-cell subset; its cell
identifiers are listed in the code repository. These files store
log-normalised expression (log1p of counts per 10,000) in their main
matrix and the original integer counts in their raw matrix. All MaxToki
analyses and the co-expression matrix read the raw counts; the scGPT and
STATE inputs are described below. Contexts are cell types: the 12
largest cell types with at least 250 cells (macrophage, pulmonary
alveolar type 2 cell, kidney epithelial cell, capillary endothelial
cell, CD4-positive alpha-beta T cell, CD8-positive alpha-beta T cell, B
cell, basal cell, pulmonary alveolar type 1 cell, intermediate monocyte,
neutrophil and classical monocyte), with 1000 randomly chosen cells per
cell type.

For independent replication we used all 5780 cells of the human CD34+
bone-marrow data of Setty et al. (Setty et al. 2019) as distributed with
scVelo (Bergen et al. 2020; {scVelo project}, n.d.), which holds
velocyto spliced and unspliced integer counts for 14,319 pre-filtered
genes (11,842 in the MaxToki vocabulary); its main matrix holds the
spliced counts only. Because the Tabula Sapiens counts include intronic
reads, the main Setty analysis ranks genes by the sum of spliced and
unspliced counts; a robustness run used spliced counts only. Contexts
are the nine differentiation clusters with at least 130 cells: two
haematopoietic stem cell (HSC) clusters, precursors, common lymphoid
progenitors (CLP), two erythroid clusters, two monocyte clusters and
dendritic cells (DC). Megakaryocytes (61 cells) were excluded. All cells
of each kept cluster were used.

Gene Ontology (GO) annotations ({Ashburner et al.} 2000; {Gene Ontology
Consortium et al.} 2023) were taken from the gene-to-GO file distributed
with GEARS (Roohani et al. 2024, 2022). A gene belongs to a functional
pole only if it is annotated directly to one of the listed GO terms;
annotations were not propagated through the GO hierarchy. Curated
transcription-factor (TF) targets were all human TRRUST v2 interactions
({Han et al.} 2018b, 2018a) together with DoRothEA interactions of
confidence A or B (Garcia-Alonso et al. 2019; Saez-Rodriguez Lab, n.d.).
Gene symbols were mapped to Ensembl identifiers with the Geneformer
dictionaries (version gc104M) (Theodoris et al. 2023; Theodoris, n.d.).

\subsection{Input encoding and extraction of contextual gene
representations}\label{input-encoding-and-extraction-of-contextual-gene-representations}

MaxToki uses Geneformer's rank-value encoding (Theodoris et al. 2023): a
cell's counts are normalised to the cell total, each gene is divided by
its median value from the model's gene-median dictionary, and the
non-zero genes are ranked in decreasing order. A per-cell scale factor
does not change the order within a cell, so the order is that of raw
count divided by gene median. The first 1022 genes were kept and wrapped
in the beginning- and end-of-sequence tokens (1024 tokens at most). On
real cells, our implementation gives the same token sequences as the
MaxToki tokenizer, except that genes with exactly tied count-to-median
values can appear in swapped order.

We recorded, for each gene in a panel, its hidden state at that gene's
token position and averaged it over the cells of one cell type. The main
MaxToki-217M extraction used layers 0, 4, 8 and 11. A separate
extraction of all 12 hidden states (the embedding output and all 11
transformer layers) with a 4000-gene panel, on the same cells, gave the
full depth profile. Four design choices each fix a failure we measured
in a pilot. (1) Pairwise contexts, not the all-context intersection: in
the main extraction only 137 genes are count-balanced in all 12 cell
types, many of them broadly expressed housekeeping genes (the
single-cell analogue of the ``stopwords'' that are most
context-sensitive in language models (Ethayarajh 2019)), whereas each
pair of cell types shares a median of 1391 count-balanced genes. The
panel contains genes with at least 25 occurrences in at least two cell
types (the 6000 most frequent such genes). (2) Cell-level split halves:
each cell was assigned to one of two partitions, so every per-gene mean
is estimated twice from disjoint cells and gives an honest noise floor.
(3) An occurrence cap: a per-gene mean has a standard error proportional
to 1/√\emph{n}, and unequal counts inject context-dependent noise into
any interaction term, so at most 50 occurrences were accumulated per
(gene, cell type, partition), over a shuffled cell order. A (gene, cell
type) entry is used only if it reached the cap in both partitions
(``count-balanced''). (4) Per-dimension z-scoring: before any cosine,
every hidden dimension was standardised over all count-balanced entries,
to remove the inflation by a few dominant (``rogue'') dimensions that a
scalar anisotropy correction misses ({Timkey and van Schijndel} 2021).

Other extractions used the same procedure with these settings. Scaling
pair: MaxToki-217M and MaxToki-1B on the same 600 cells per cell type
with a 5000-gene panel (cap 50), at layers 2 and 4 of MaxToki-217M and
layers 4 and 7 of MaxToki-1B, so that the models can be compared at
matched relative depth (layer 4 of 11 = 0.36 versus layer 7 of 20 =
0.35; layer 2 of 11 = 0.18 versus layer 4 of 20 = 0.20). Random-weights
control: the same cells and the same 5000-gene panel as the MaxToki-217M
scaling run, at layers 4 and 8. Cap-matched control: the first 20
occurrences of each (gene, cell type, partition) in the main
extraction's forward pass, at layer 4. Setty data: cap 25, a floor of 15
occurrences in at least two clusters, a 5000-gene panel, layers 2 and 4.
scGPT: representations from an existing per-gene activation atlas of
3000 Tabula Sapiens cells (immune, kidney and lung) (Kendiukhov 2026e),
in which each cell's 1200 highest-expressed genes were given to scGPT
with their log-normalised values passed to the value channel without
scGPT's standard binning; we reformatted it into the same layout: cell
types with at least 100 cells (8 cell types), two partitions, cap 20,
floor 12, 5000 genes. STATE: a preprocessed copy of each tissue that
keeps about 4800 highly variable genes, with ambient-corrected counts;
600 cells per tissue (immune, kidney and lung), taken as up to 150 cells
from each of the largest cell types until 600 were reached (in practice
the 4 largest per tissue); STATE fills its input with unexpressed genes
as well, and we kept only positions holding expressed genes (count
\textgreater{} 0); cell types with at least 100 cells (9 cell types),
cap 20, floor 12 (1840 genes).

\subsection{Two-way decomposition and contextualisation strength
(EXCESS)}\label{two-way-decomposition-and-contextualisation-strength-excess}

For gene \emph{g} in cell type \emph{c}, we write \emph{v(g,c) = μ +
a(g) + b(c) + E(g,c)}: a gene main effect \emph{a(g)} (the gene's
identity), a context main effect \emph{b(c)} (a shift shared by all
genes in that cell type, expected because attention pools over the
cell), and a gene-specific interaction \emph{E(g,c)}, the only term
where a gene-specific response to context can appear.

For a cell-type pair (\emph{c}\textsubscript{0},
\emph{c}\textsubscript{1}), the relative shift of gene \emph{g} is
δ(\emph{g}) = {[}\emph{v}(\emph{g},\emph{c}\textsubscript{1}) −
\emph{v}(\emph{g},\emph{c}\textsubscript{0}){]} −
mean\textsubscript{\emph{g′}}{[}\emph{v}(\emph{g′},\emph{c}\textsubscript{1})
− \emph{v}(\emph{g′},\emph{c}\textsubscript{0}){]}: the gene's own shift
minus the average shift of all genes. We computed δ separately in the
two cell partitions (δ\textsubscript{0}, δ\textsubscript{1}) and defined
EXCESS = mean cos(δ\textsubscript{0}(\emph{g}),
δ\textsubscript{1}(\emph{g})) − mean cos(δ\textsubscript{0}(\emph{g}),
δ\textsubscript{1}(perm \emph{g})): the same gene's shift agreeing
across independent cells, minus a gene-shuffled control. EXCESS = 0
means no gene-specific response to context. The means are pooled over
all (gene, pair) entries from the cell-type pairs with at least 200
count-balanced genes (150 in the cross-model and Setty analyses), which
equals a mean over pairs weighted by their number of genes. 95\%
confidence intervals (CIs) come from 2000 bootstrap resamples of genes,
in which each resampled gene brings all of its pair entries. As a
positive control we report the replication of the context main effect
across partitions (the cosine between the two partitions' mean shifts).

\textbf{Anisotropy.} On the raw (unstandardised) representations we
computed the mean cosine of random different-gene pairs (the anisotropy
baseline (Ethayarajh 2019)), same-gene self-similarity across cell
types, and the share of variance on the first principal component (PC),
and repeated these after per-dimension z-scoring.

\textbf{Rank-position control.} MaxToki encodes token position, and a
gene's rank changes between cell types. We computed each gene's mean
rank in each cell type over the cells that express it, using the same
cells as the extraction. We split genes at the median absolute rank
change into rank-stable and rank-moving halves and recomputed EXCESS in
each, correlated the size of the shift with the rank change, and
recomputed EXCESS after regressing the shift on the gene's mean rank in
each of the two cell types and on its absolute rank change.

\subsection{Functional axes and reproducible interaction
power}\label{functional-axes-and-reproducible-interaction-power}

A functional axis \emph{u} is the unit vector separating two GO-defined
gene classes in the gene main effect, \emph{u} ∝ mean(\emph{a}(\emph{g})
\textbar{} class A) − mean(\emph{a}(\emph{g}) \textbar{} class B), where
\emph{a}(\emph{g}) is the gene's z-scored representation averaged over
partitions and count-balanced cell types. Genes annotated to both poles
were removed from both. The axes were nuclear versus surface/secreted
(nucleus GO:0005634, chromatin GO:0000785 and DNA binding GO:0003677,
versus plasma membrane GO:0005886, extracellular region GO:0005576 and
extracellular space GO:0005615); mitochondrion (GO:0005739) versus
cytoskeleton (GO:0005856); transcription (regulation of DNA-templated
transcription GO:0006355 and DNA-binding transcription factor activity
GO:0003700) versus transport and signalling (ion transport GO:0006811
and signalling receptor activity GO:0038023); and cell cycle
(GO:0007049) versus cell differentiation (GO:0030154). Axis validity is
the five-fold cross-validated area under the receiver operating
characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) with which ⟨\emph{a}(\emph{g}),
\emph{u}⟩ separates held-out pole genes.

Reproducible interaction power measures how much context moves genes
along \emph{u} in a way that replicates across independent cells. For
genes count-balanced in at least 9 cell types, we projected each
partition's representation onto \emph{u},
\emph{q}\textsubscript{\emph{p}}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) =
⟨\emph{v}\textsubscript{\emph{p}}(\emph{g},\emph{c}), \emph{u}⟩;
regressed out the gene's mean rank in that cell type (linear and
quadratic terms; ``rank-controlled''); took the two-way interaction
\emph{I}\textsubscript{\emph{p}}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) (the projection
minus its gene mean and context mean, plus the grand mean); and averaged
\emph{I}\textsubscript{0}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) ·
\emph{I}\textsubscript{1}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) over all (gene, cell type)
entries. Noise averages to zero in this product, so only modulation that
reproduces across the two partitions survives. Functional-z is the
z-score of this power against 300 axes built from random gene partitions
of the same pole sizes. We report it at layers 4, 8 and 11, without and
with the rank regression. A cell type's loading on an axis is the mean
projection onto \emph{u} of the z-scored layer-4 representations
(averaged over partitions) of the genes count-balanced in that cell
type. These gene sets differ between cell types and the loadings are not
rank-controlled, so we use them only to describe the ordering of cell
types.

\subsection{Null models for functional
organisation}\label{null-models-for-functional-organisation}

Functional gene sets may be co-expressed, and co-expressed genes co-vary
across cells whatever the model ``understands''. We therefore compared
each functional axis's power with null axes whose two poles have the
same sizes as the functional poles. Co-expression was the Pearson
correlation of log1p(counts per 10,000), computed from the raw counts
over 3000 cells from each of the three Tabula Sapiens datasets, and the
coherence of a gene set was its mean pairwise correlation.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Random axes:} random genes (300 axes).
\item
  \textbf{Strongly co-expressed modules:} each null pole was the set of
  genes most correlated with a random seed gene (300 axes). These
  modules are much more co-expressed than the functional poles
  (Results), so this is a strong null rather than a matched one.
\item
  \textbf{Size- and co-expression-matched sets:} each null pole was
  built from the \emph{k} genes most correlated with a random seed gene
  plus randomly chosen genes, with \emph{k} chosen by bisection so that
  the pole's coherence was within 10\% of the functional pole's (300
  axes). We also traced power against the fraction of seed-co-expressed
  genes at fixed pole size (0 to 100\%, 25 draws per step).
\item
  \textbf{Size- and tightness-matched sets:} the same construction with
  representation tightness (the mean pairwise cosine of the pole genes'
  main effects \emph{a}(\emph{g})) in place of co-expression (300 axes).
\end{enumerate}

For each null, the empirical one-sided p is (\emph{k} + 1)/(\emph{N} +
1), where \emph{k} is the number of the \emph{N} = 300 null axes with
power at least as large as the functional axis; when no null axis
reached it, p takes its smallest possible value, 1/301. Empirical p
values were used because the null power distributions are right-skewed.

\subsection{Causal steering test}\label{causal-steering-test}

To test whether the model uses the functional direction, we added it to
the residual stream at half of a cell's gene positions and read the
model's own next-gene output logits at the other half. The direction was
the raw (unstandardised) difference between the mean layer-4
representations of the nuclear-pole and surface-pole genes (poles made
disjoint, as for the axes). It was added at the layer-4 representation
(the output of the fourth transformer block). For each of 30 immune
cells (at most 512 tokens), gene positions were randomly split into a
steered half and a read half, so any effect at the read positions must
travel through attention. The readout was the mean change in logit of
nuclear-pole gene tokens minus that of surface-pole gene tokens
(specificity). Strengths were 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 times the mean
residual-stream norm at that layer in one reference cell; we report 0.5
as the headline. Pushing toward +\emph{u} and toward −\emph{u} and
taking the difference (the signed swing) cancels any effect that does
not depend on the sign of the push. The control was a random direction
of the same norm, drawn fresh for each cell. Significance was a
one-sided sign test of functional swing greater than random swing across
cells. The test was repeated at the layer-8 representation and for the
mitochondrion/cytoskeleton and transcription/transport axes at layer 4.
To put the push sizes in context, we measured how far context naturally
moves genes along the same direction: the spread of each gene's
projection onto \emph{u} across its count-balanced cell types.

\subsection{Tests for context-specific gene
function}\label{tests-for-context-specific-gene-function}

\textbf{Directional congruence.} Along each functional axis, we
decomposed each gene's projection into a gene loading \emph{f}(\emph{g})
(mean over contexts), a context loading \emph{h}(\emph{c}) (mean over
genes) and an interaction \emph{e}(\emph{g},\emph{c}). If genes move
toward their own functional pole specifically in cell types that share
that character, \emph{e} should follow \emph{f}·\emph{h}. We estimated
\emph{f} and \emph{h} in one partition and \emph{e} in the other (and
vice versa), measured β = corr(\emph{e}, \emph{f}·\emph{h}) on
rank-residualised projections, and compared β with 300 null axes whose
poles were matched to the functional poles on size and co-expression (as
in null 3 above).

\textbf{Placement near curated targets.} For TFs count-balanced in at
least 6 cell types with at least 15 curated targets in the gene panel,
we asked (A) whether, in each cell type, the TF's representation (with
the context mean removed) is closer by cosine to the centroid of its
curated targets than to the centroid of non-targets matched on
co-expression with the TF (40 redraws), and (B) whether this excess
closeness rises in cell types where the targets are more highly
expressed (Spearman correlation across cell types with target activity,
the negative of the targets' mean rank). A cell type was used for a TF
only if at least 8 of its targets were count-balanced there, and a TF
needed at least 3 such cell types. Both questions were tested across TFs
with sign tests.

\textbf{Lineage transcription factors.} We also attempted a test on 14
TFs with lineage-specific roles in immune cells (SPI1, IRF4, IRF8,
GATA3, MAFB, BCL6, PRDM1, TBX21, RUNX1, RUNX3, BATF, MAF, BACH2 and
ZEB2) (Heinz et al. 2010), but few of them were sampled well enough in
the panel (Results).

\subsection{Cross-model, scaling and random-weights
comparison}\label{cross-model-scaling-and-random-weights-comparison}

EXCESS and nuclear/surface functional-z were computed identically for
every model at layer 4, and for the scaling pair also at matched
relative depth, on each model's own gene vocabulary (context pairs with
at least 150 count-balanced genes). In this comparison, and in the Setty
replication, functional-z was computed without the rank regression, on
genes count-balanced in at least ⌊\emph{n}/2⌋ of the \emph{n} cell types
(4 of 9 for STATE and the Setty clusters), against 200 random-partition
axes. Because the occurrence cap differs between architectures (50 for
MaxToki, 20 for scGPT and STATE), we also report MaxToki-217M with cap
20.

\subsection{Link to next-gene
prediction}\label{link-to-next-gene-prediction}

For 350 Tabula Sapiens immune cells with at least 60 expressed
vocabulary genes (at most 512 tokens), and up to 20 target positions per
cell from the sixth gene onward, we compared the model's log-probability
of the true gene given the cell's own preceding genes with that given
the same number of preceding genes from another cell (a chimeric
context). The donor cell was drawn at random among cells with at least
as many genes whose preceding genes did not include the target gene, so
that the chimeric context never repeats the target. The per-gene context
benefit (log-probability difference, averaged over occurrences; genes
with at least 3 occurrences) was correlated with the gene's per-gene
context movement, the mean over its count-balanced cell types (at least
4) of ‖\emph{z}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) − mean\textsubscript{\emph{c}}
\emph{z}(\emph{g},\emph{c})‖ in the z-scored layer-4 space (a per-gene
quantity, distinct from EXCESS). We used a partial Spearman correlation
controlling for log gene frequency, with a 95\% CI from 2000 bootstrap
resamples of genes.

\subsection{Statistics and
reproducibility}\label{statistics-and-reproducibility}

All analyses are deterministic (random seed 0) and run on cached
extractions. Empirical p values are one-sided unless stated. Multiple
tests were corrected within each family: the four steering

\texttt{ctx\_independent.py} (Setty replication);
\texttt{ctx\_functional\_axes.py}, \texttt{ctx\_celltype\_loadings.py},

\section{Results}\label{results}

All analyses below use MaxToki-217M unless a model is named. We first
establish Level 1 (contextualisation is real, not rank position,
replicable, partly learned and weakly linked to prediction, and one
functional direction is read by the model when pushed), then report the
Level 2 tests.

\subsection{Genes are contextualised gene-specifically, most strongly in
the first
layers}\label{genes-are-contextualised-gene-specifically-most-strongly-in-the-first-layers}

The gene-specific context response is large and highly reproducible
(Table 1, Fig. 1). At layer 4 of the main extraction, EXCESS is +0.758:
the same gene's relative shift agrees at cosine +0.759 across
independent cells, while a different gene's agrees at +0.001, so the
effect is gene-specific. The context main effect replicates at +0.996,
which shows that the measurement works. Layer 0 (the context-free
embedding output) returns exactly 0.000, the check that the pipeline
reports nothing when there is nothing. The full depth scan shows where
the response arises: it is highest at layer 1 (+0.847) and layer 2
(+0.835) and then falls to +0.651 at layer 11 (Fig. 1). Context is
therefore integrated in the first one or two transformer layers. Deeper
layers keep most of it (90\% of the peak at layer 4 and 84\% at layer
8), while the gene-specific shift becomes somewhat less reproducible
toward the output.

\subsection{The effect is not the gene's rank
position}\label{the-effect-is-not-the-genes-rank-position}

MaxToki reads a cell as a rank-ordered gene list and encodes token
position, so a gene ranking 5th in one cell type and 500th in another is
an obvious confound. It is not the driver (Fig. 2). The gene-specific
response is about the same for rank-stable genes (+0.749) and
rank-moving genes (+0.767) at layer 4, and 99\% of it survives
regression on rank (+0.749); 98\% and 97\% survive at layers 8 and 11.
The size of a gene's shift correlates only weakly with its rank change
(ρ = 0.26).

\subsection{The effect replicates on an independent
dataset}\label{the-effect-replicates-on-an-independent-dataset}

On the Setty CD34+ bone-marrow data, the lineage in which a recoverable
manifold was found in scGPT (Kendiukhov 2026a), with nine
differentiation clusters rather than adult cell types as contexts, the
phenomenon replicates: EXCESS is +0.713 at layer 2 and +0.713 at layer 4
(gene-shuffled null +0.0036 and +0.0039), and functional-z is +5.0 and
+5.0 for the nuclear/surface axis and +16.1 and +8.8 for the
mitochondrion/cytoskeleton axis. Using spliced counts only gives the
same picture (EXCESS +0.702 and nuclear/surface functional-z +9.3 at
layer 4). Neither the contextualisation nor its functional organisation
is specific to Tabula Sapiens or to adult cell types.

\subsection{Context moves genes along functional axes, as co-expression
does}\label{context-moves-genes-along-functional-axes-as-co-expression-does}

along random directions (Table 2). At layer 4, functional-z
(rank-controlled in parentheses) is +16.6 (+13.3) for nuclear versus
surface/secreted genes, +8.7 (+6.4) for mitochondrion versus
cytoskeleton and +2.9 (+4.3) for transcription versus transport. The
cell-cycle versus differentiation axis, which separates its poles only
weakly (validity AUC 0.57), gives +2.0 (+0.9). The organisation persists
in deeper layers for the two strongest axes. Cell types order sensibly
along the nuclear/surface axis: lymphocytes sit at the nuclear end (CD8
T +4.7, CD4 T +4.4, B +3.9) and alveolar epithelial cells and
macrophages at the surface end (alveolar type 1 −3.6, type 2 −3.2,
macrophage −2.8).

\subsection{The nuclear/surface direction is read by the model, but not
every functional direction
is}\label{the-nuclearsurface-direction-is-read-by-the-model-but-not-every-functional-direction-is}

In the steering test (Fig. 4), adding the nuclear/surface direction to
half of a cell's genes moves the model's own logits at the untouched
half with the sign of the push: nuclear-gene logits rise for a push
toward the nuclear pole (specificity +0.085) and fall for a push toward
the surface pole (−0.097), a signed swing of +0.182 against +0.001 for a
random direction of the same norm. The swing is +0.079, +0.182, +0.184
at strengths 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 and exceeds the random control in 22 of
30 cells (p = 0.0081; Bonferroni-adjusted 0.032). The effect is stronger
at the layer-8 representation (swing +0.232, 30 of 30 cells, p = 9.3 ×
10\textsuperscript{−10}). The direction therefore propagates through
attention and changes the model's predictions.

This does not generalise to every functional axis. The
mitochondrion/cytoskeleton direction has no significant effect at
strengths 0.25 and 0.5 (swing +0.024, +0.067, +0.269; at 0.5, 15 of 30
cells, p = 0.57); at strength 1.0 it exceeds the random control in 20 of
30 cells (p = 0.049, Bonferroni-adjusted 0.20), the same as the
nuclear/surface direction at that strength. The transcription/transport
direction has a reversed effect that grows with strength (swing −0.139,
−0.268, −0.678): the functional swing exceeds the random one in only 2
of 30 cells (two-sided sign test p = 8.7 × 10\textsuperscript{−7}). We
have no explanation for the reversal; it shows that an axis that
separates genes is not automatically read in the expected direction. The
pushes are also large compared with natural variation: the middle
strength (7.6 units) is about 3 times the median range of a gene's
projection on the nuclear/surface direction across cell types (2.62).
The test therefore shows that the model can read this axis, not that the
natural, context-driven changes along it are used.

\subsection{No context-specific gene function beyond
co-expression}\label{no-context-specific-gene-function-beyond-co-expression}

Two direct tests for context-appropriate gene function find no
context-specific signal beyond co-expression. (1) Directional
congruence: genes do not move toward their own functional pole
specifically in cell types that share that character more than matched
null axes do. At layer 4, β is +0.16, +0.10 and +0.23 for the three
axes, all positive but within the upper tail of size- and
co-expression-matched null axes, whose means are near zero (p = 0.116,
0.086 and 0.053; none significant after Holm correction over 6 tests; at
layer 8, β is −0.16, −0.11 and +0.09). The null spread of β (standard
deviation 0.08--0.13) means the test could detect only clear congruence.
(2) Placement near curated targets: across 26 TFs, at layer 4 a TF's
representation is slightly closer to the centroid of its curated targets
than to that of co-expression-matched non-targets (mean cosine excess
+0.021; 20 of 26 TFs positive; sign test p = 0.005, Bonferroni-adjusted
over 4 tests 0.019). This closeness does not rise significantly in the
cell types where the targets are more active (mean ρ = +0.073; 17 of 26
TFs positive; p = 0.08, Bonferroni-adjusted 0.34), and at layer 8
neither effect is significant (static p = 0.279; modulation ρ = +0.135,
p = 0.08). So the model places TFs modestly near their curated targets
beyond what co-expression with the TF predicts, but not in a
context-appropriate way. A third attempt, on 14 lineage TFs, is
uninformative rather than negative: only 1 of them (RUNX1) was sampled
well enough, because these regulators are weakly expressed in the cells
we used.

\subsection{Contextualisation as a model metric: architecture, scale,
and learned versus
architectural}\label{contextualisation-as-a-model-metric-architecture-scale-and-learned-versus-architectural}

Because it does not depend on resolving gene function, contextualisation
can serve as a model metric (Table 3, Fig. 5). Three findings stand out.
(1) Every model contextualises genes strongly (EXCESS 0.71--0.83), but
contextualisation does not grow consistently with model size. At matched
relative depth, MaxToki-1B gives +0.767 (layer 7) against +0.740 for
MaxToki-217M (layer 4), and +0.767 (layer 4) against +0.831 (layer 2).
Functional organisation is not higher in the larger model either
(functional-z +8.1 against +14.3, and +18.4 against +18.4). (2)
Functional organisation differs sharply between architectures: the
rank-based MaxToki models give z = 8.1--18.4 (Table 3), scGPT +3.2 and
STATE +1.1. STATE moves genes strongly in context (EXCESS +0.828) but in
no functionally coherent direction; its gene tokens are fixed protein
embeddings rather than learned from co-expression. The models differ in
more than tokenisation (training data, objective, gene panel and context
sets), so this comparison does not isolate the cause. (3) The phenomenon
is partly architectural and partly learned. An untrained MaxToki of the
same architecture, run on the same cells and gene panel, keeps about
60\% of the trained contextualisation (EXCESS +0.441 against +0.740) but
only about a third of its functional organisation (functional-z +4.7
against +14.3). Fixed random embeddings and attention mixing give a
reproducible, gene-specific shift on their own; training strengthens it
and aligns it with functional axes. The untrained MaxToki's functional-z
(+4.7) is higher than that of trained scGPT (+3.2) and STATE (+1.1), so
part of what this metric measures comes from MaxToki's rank-based input
and architecture rather than from training.

\subsection{Contextualisation is weakly aligned with what the model uses
for
prediction}\label{contextualisation-is-weakly-aligned-with-what-the-model-uses-for-prediction}

If contextualisation serves the autoregressive objective, the genes the
model contextualises most should be those whose next-gene prediction
gains most from real context. That is what we find. Real context helps
prediction a great deal: across 2266 comparisons for 558 genes, the mean
benefit is +12.0 nats (median +12.9; a nat is a natural-log unit of
probability), so the true gene is roughly 160,000-fold more probable
under its real context than under another cell's. The per-gene size of
this benefit tracks how much the gene moves between cell types: the
partial Spearman correlation, controlling for gene frequency, is +0.161
(95\% CI +0.083 to +0.239). Genes whose representations depend more on
context tend, weakly, to be genes whose prediction depends more on
context.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The results characterise how SCFMs represent genes in context and what
that representation carries. On the positive side, MaxToki reshapes gene
representations gene-specifically and reproducibly, most strongly in its
first one or two layers. Training strengthens the reshaping (an
untrained model keeps about 60\% of its strength and a third of its
functional organisation); the reshaping is weakly aligned, gene by gene,
with how much the model's predictions depend on context; and in the
MaxToki models it follows functional axes. To our knowledge this is the
first measurement for single-cell models of the ``how contextual are
contextualised representations'' question (Ethayarajh 2019), and the
comparison of models gives an interpretability metric that separates the
three architectures we tested, though not the two MaxToki sizes
consistently.

What the reshaping carries is, as far as our tests can tell,
co-expression. The functional organisation of the context changes is no
stronger than that of gene sets matched to the functional sets on size
and average co-expression, and modules of strongly co-expressed genes
produce more; we did not test directly whether a representation built
from expression alone reproduces it. The two direct tests for
context-specific gene function are negative. The one signal beyond
co-expression is static: TFs sit slightly closer to their curated
targets than to co-expression-matched non-targets at layer 4, but not
more so in the cell types where those targets are active. The steering
result fits the same picture. The model reads the nuclear/surface
direction, but the pushes tested (3.8--15.2 units) are 1.4 to 6 times a
gene's typical natural range along it, so the test does not show that
natural context changes are used; and two other functional directions
are not consistently read, or are read in reverse. The architecture
comparison does not contradict this: STATE, whose gene tokens are fixed
protein embeddings, contextualises genes strongly but without functional
organisation, and scGPT, also trained on expression, shows little; the
comparison cannot separate tokenisation from the other differences
between the models.

One possible reason for this boundary is that an expression-only model
sees only which genes are expressed and their rank order, and its
objective rewards predicting co-expressed genes. The alignment between
contextualisation and the benefit of real context for prediction is
consistent with that reading, although the test measures only how far a
gene moves, not in which direction, so it does not show that the model
moves genes toward their co-expressed partners. Some context-dependent
function acts at the protein level, as in moonlighting proteins (Jeffery
1999), and leaves no expression signature. Other forms, such as
cell-type-specific enhancer choice by lineage-determining TFs (Heinz et
al. 2010), act through target-gene expression and could in principle be
visible; our tests did not detect them. Whether multi-omic,
sequence-aware or larger expression models reach Level 2 is not tested
here.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

Most analyses were run on MaxToki-217M only: the rank control, the Setty
replication, the steering test, the co-expression and tightness nulls,
the prediction link and both Level 2 tests. MaxToki-1B, scGPT and STATE
were tested only for contextualisation strength and nuclear/surface
functional organisation. Cross-architecture EXCESS is confounded by the
occurrence cap (20 for scGPT and STATE versus 50 for MaxToki) and by
width. A MaxToki-217M run with the same cap of 20 gives EXCESS +0.620
and functional-z +28.0, so the functional-z gap between architectures is
not caused by the cap, but EXCESS values should not be ranked across
architectures. The functional-z comparison uses non-identical context
sets (12, 9 and 8 cell types) and, for STATE, a smaller gene panel (1840
genes) and only 14 of 36 cell-type pairs with enough count-balanced
genes; we did not re-run it on a common context set. STATE was given
only about 4800 highly variable genes per tissue (ambient-corrected
counts), which is likely one reason its panel holds only 1840 genes and
few cell-type pairs could be scored. The rank control was run only for
MaxToki; scGPT and STATE also receive each gene's expression level, so
part of their EXCESS may reflect the gene's own expression change rather
than its context. The scGPT representations come from an existing
extraction in which log-normalised values were given to scGPT's value
channel without its standard binning, so its absolute activations are
off its training distribution; our analyses use contrasts that largely
cancel this, but the scGPT numbers should be read as relative to
MaxToki, not as calibrated values. The scaling comparison has two model
sizes only.

Most analyses use layer 4, not the peak layers 1--2; the co-expression
nulls were run only at layer 4 and the tightness null at layers 4 and 8.
The steering direction is the context-averaged nuclear-minus-surface
direction, a gene-class direction rather than the context-specific
change itself, and it was compared only with a random push, not with
co-expression-matched directions. The co-expression nulls match average
pairwise co-expression; power also depends on how co-expression is
distributed within a gene set, and we did not run the more direct test
of whether a representation built only from expression (for example, a
factorisation of the same cells) reproduces the functional organisation.
The Setty data cover a pre-filtered set of genes, unlike the full raw
counts of Tabula Sapiens. The Level 2 negatives are evidence of a
ceiling, not proof of absence: some context-dependent function is
invisible to an expression-only model, and the directional test could
detect only clear congruence. Functional axes are built from direct
annotations in one annotation file; the conclusions hold across three
axes, but specific z-scores depend on how the poles are defined.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

Single-cell foundation models build contextual gene representations that
are a genuine object of study: a gene's vector is reshaped by its
cellular context in a gene-specific and reproducible way that training
strengthens, most strongly in the first layers, weakly aligned with how
much the model's predictions rely on context, and different between
architectures. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic
characterisation of the phenomenon for single-cell models. The
functional organisation of the contextual change gives a simple metric
that separates the three architectures we tested. In MaxToki, controls
matched on co-expression also bound what the contextualisation carries:
its functional organisation is no stronger than that of gene sets with
the same co-expression, and two direct tests for context-specific gene
function are negative. Reading a gene's changed role in a new cell type,
the use that would make these representations a hypothesis-generation
tool, is not reachable with the expression-only model tested here and
will likely require models trained on more than expression.
