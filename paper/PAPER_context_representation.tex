\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{Background:} Single-cell foundation models build, at every
transformer layer, a representation of each gene that depends on the
other genes in the cell. Whether this contextual representation shows
how a gene's role differs between cell types, the single-cell
counterpart of word sense in language models, has not been tested.

\textbf{Results:} We measured contextual gene representations in MaxToki
(217M and 1B), scGPT and STATE on identical Tabula Sapiens cells from 12
cell types, and in MaxToki-217M also on independent bone-marrow data. In
every model a gene's representation changes with cell type in a
gene-specific way: the change agrees between two independent halves of
the cells, with a cosine 0.74 to 0.87 higher than for a randomly paired
gene. It is not explained by the gene's rank in the cell and replicates
on the bone-marrow data. Untrained versions keep 36--88\% of it, and a
representation built from expression alone also shows it (+0.321). In
MaxToki-217M the change follows functional axes such as nuclear versus
surface genes (z = +13.3 against random axes; on this axis STATE shows
less and scGPT little). In all four models it follows co-expressed gene
sets. None of three expression-only representations shows either kind of
organisation. In no model is the functional organisation significantly
stronger than that of gene sets matched on co-expression (smallest p =
0.07), although this test detects only large differences. Adding the
nuclear/surface direction to half of a cell's genes shifts MaxToki's
predictions for the other half more than random directions from the same
genes; as the pushes exceed natural changes, this shows the model can
read the direction, not that it uses it. In both MaxToki models, genes
that change more gain slightly more from their own context in next-gene
prediction. Two direct tests for context-specific gene function were
negative in all four models.

\textbf{Conclusions:} Single-cell foundation models build real
contextual gene representations, organised by co-expression. We found no
context-specific gene function beyond co-expression, though our tests
could detect only clear effects. Models trained on more than expression
were not tested. Functional organisation separates some architectures
but does not rank them reliably.

\textbf{Keywords:} Single-cell foundation models; Contextual gene
representations; Mechanistic interpretability; Gene co-expression; Gene
embeddings; Transformers; Single-cell RNA sequencing; Model evaluation

\section{Background}\label{background}

A recurring theme in the interpretability of large language models is
that a network trained on a narrow predictive task builds internal
representations of structure it was never taught. Sequence models
trained on Othello and chess build internal representations of the board
and of hidden variables such as player skill (Li et al. 2023; Nanda et
al. 2023; Karvonen 2024). Language models encode space and time along
single directions that a linear probe can read (Gurnee and Tegmark
2024). In the best cases the model also uses these representations to
make its predictions. One tests this by changing the representation and
measuring the effect on the model's output (Turner et al. 2023; {Zou et
al.} 2023; Park et al. 2024).

A closely related line concerns contextual representations. Contextual
word embeddings assign the same word a different vector in every
sentence, and the geometry of those vectors encodes word sense: ``bank''
near a river and ``bank'' in finance occupy separable regions (Peters et
al. 2018; Ethayarajh 2019; Coenen et al. 2019). These measurements need
care, because representation spaces are strongly anisotropic (vectors
cluster in a narrow cone rather than spreading evenly, so any two look
similar) and a handful of ``rogue'' dimensions can dominate cosine
similarity (Ethayarajh 2019; Mu et al. 2018; Cai et al. 2021; {Timkey
and van Schijndel} 2021).

Single-cell foundation models (SCFMs) offer a clean biological version
of the same question (Theodoris et al. 2023; Cui et al. 2024; Hao et al.
2024; Yang et al. 2022). They treat each gene as a token and each cell
as a set or sequence of genes, either ordered by expression (Geneformer)
or paired with binned or continuous expression values (scGPT, scBERT,
scFoundation), and are trained to predict masked or next genes or
values, in effect learning which genes tend to be active together. Their
gene token embeddings (one fixed vector per gene) have been studied (Cui
et al. 2024; Kendiukhov 2026f); their contextual gene representations (a
gene's vector inside a particular cell) are used as features downstream
(Hao et al. 2024; Bian et al. 2024). Their geometry has been mapped when
averaged over cells or within one tissue (Related work), but how a
single gene's vector changes from one cell type to another has not been
measured. A model could use this vector to represent that a gene plays a
different role in a macrophage than in a T cell. That would be the
biological counterpart of word sense. The ``cell as a token'' framing
points to this question but does not test it (Gilpin 2025).

\textbf{Related work.} Our methods descend directly from the
language-model literature on contextualised embeddings. Ethayarajh
(Ethayarajh 2019) showed that contextual representations are anisotropic
in all non-input layers, that same-word self-similarity falls in upper
layers, and that every cosine must be corrected against an anisotropy
baseline. Timkey and van Schijndel ({Timkey and van Schijndel} 2021)
showed that a few rogue dimensions dominate similarity and that a scalar
correction is not enough; Mu et al. (Mu et al. 2018) and Cai et al. (Cai
et al. 2021) give the standard postprocessing and the local-isotropy
picture. Coenen et al. (Coenen et al. 2019) and Garí Soler and
Apidianaki (Garí Soler and Apidianaki 2021) show that word senses form
separable clusters and that a word's degree of polysemy is itself
readable. We import both the measurements and the controls. From the
literature on latent variables that models use causally (Li et al. 2023;
Nanda et al. 2023; Karvonen 2024; Gurnee and Tegmark 2024) we adopt one
rule: a probe shows only correlation, so a causal claim needs an
intervention compared against a matched control. We also use the
steering methods of this literature (Turner et al. 2023; {Zou et al.}
2023). Templeton et al.~did the same at large scale, extracting
interpretable feature directions from a production model and steering it
with them ({Templeton et al.} 2024).

Geneformer (Theodoris et al. 2023), scGPT (Cui et al. 2024),
scFoundation (Hao et al. 2024), scBERT (Yang et al. 2022), Universal
Cell Embeddings (UCE) (Rosen et al. 2026) and STATE ({Adduri et al.}
2026) differ in tokenisation (rank-ordered gene identity, binned or
continuous expression values, or gene tokens derived from
protein-sequence embeddings), and the review of Bian et al. (Bian et al.
2024) defines the ``contextual gene embedding'' that our work dissects.
MaxToki ({Gómez Ortega et al.} 2026), the primary model here, is a
Llama-architecture, Geneformer-lineage model at 217M and 1B scales. Two
earlier analyses come closest to a ``context changes the gene'' claim.
scGPT reports cell-state-specific gene networks read from attention (Cui
et al. 2024), but as a pairwise network, not as a property of the
representation vector, and without a decomposition or a null.
Geneformer's in silico deletion measures how the embeddings of the other
genes shift when one gene is removed from a cell (Theodoris et al.
2023), which is a change of context by one gene. Neither characterises
how a gene's representation differs between natural cell types. The
geometry of gene representations in these models has been mapped, both
for static gene embeddings (Kendiukhov 2026f) and for layer-wise hidden
states averaged over cells, including their spectral structure
(Kendiukhov 2026b) and the topological and geometric structure they do
and do not encode, per tissue (Kendiukhov 2026g). Residual-stream gene
vectors built separately in several tissues carry a small,
tissue-dependent amount of transcription-factor-target signal beyond
expression covariates (Kendiukhov 2026c). That test used TRRUST edges,
which are the same in every tissue, so it did not ask whether a gene's
role differs between tissues.

A critical literature finds that SCFMs often fail to beat simple
baselines: on zero-shot tasks (Kedzierska et al. 2025), under deeper
evaluation (Boiarsky et al. 2024), on perturbation prediction
(Ahlmann-Eltze et al. 2025; Bendidi et al. 2024), and across utilities
(Liu et al. 2026). Sparse autoencoders find organised biological
knowledge but minimal regulatory logic in these models (Kendiukhov
2026d), and edge scores read from SCFM attention add nothing beyond
simple gene-level statistics for predicting which genes respond to a
perturbation (Kendiukhov 2026e). Co-expression is the canonical
guilt-by-association signal ({van Dam et al.} 2018), and some
gene-embedding methods, such as Gene2vec, are built directly on it (Du
et al. 2019); others build gene embeddings from text descriptions of
genes instead (Chen and Zou 2025). Genuine context-dependent gene
function exists. Some of it acts at the protein level, as when a
moonlighting protein changes function with its location or binding
partners (Jeffery 1999), and need not change the gene's expression. Some
acts through transcription, as when lineage-determining transcription
factors select different enhancers with different partners by cell type
(Heinz et al. 2010).

\textbf{Aim.} We organise the question into two levels. Level 1 (a fact
about the model): is a gene's representation reshaped by context in a
structured, gene-specific way, is that reshaping learned, and is it
linked to what the model does? Level 2 (biology): does the reshaping
reveal something about the gene's role in each context beyond what
co-expression already tells us? We study Level 1 for MaxToki-217M in
depth (rank control, independent replication, random-weights control,
link to the model's predictions, steering) and repeat the central tests
in MaxToki-1B, scGPT and STATE on identical cells. For Level 2, we test
the functional organisation of the contextualisation against
co-expression nulls and against representations built from expression
alone, and run two direct tests for context-specific gene function, in
all four models. The study makes three contributions. (1) It measures
how much, and how gene-specifically, a gene's representation changes
between cell types (Level 1). It also tests whether the model reacts
when functional directions are pushed into it, and whether genes that
change more gain more from their own context in next-gene prediction.
Neither test shows that the natural change is used. (2) It shows that
this change is organised by co-expression, and that two tests find no
context-specific gene function beyond co-expression in any of the four
models (Level 2). (3) It tests whether the functional organisation of
the change can be used to compare models, and where that comparison
breaks down.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Models}\label{models}

MaxToki-217M and MaxToki-1B ({Gómez Ortega et al.} 2026; Theodoris Lab
2026) are Llama-architecture autoregressive models over rank-ordered
gene tokens, with 11 and 20 transformer layers and hidden widths of 1232
and 2304. We used the Hugging Face safetensors checkpoints through the
\texttt{transformers} library with \texttt{output\_hidden\_states=True},
in float32. For MaxToki, ``layer \emph{k}'' is the \emph{k}-th hidden
state: layer 0 is the embedding output and layer \emph{k} ≥ 1 is the
output of the \emph{k}-th transformer block; the last hidden state
(layer 11 of MaxToki-217M) is taken after the model's final
normalisation. scGPT (Cui et al. 2024; Bo Wang Lab, n.d.) (whole-human
checkpoint; 12 layers; width 512) and STATE ({Adduri et al.} 2026; Arc
Institute 2025) (the SE-600M state-embedding model; 16 layers; width
2048) were used for the cross-architecture comparison. STATE's gene
tokens are fixed protein embeddings from the Evolutionary Scale Modeling
2 (ESM2) language model ({Lin et al.} 2023), passed through a learned
projection together with each gene's expression. For all models, ``layer
\emph{k}'' is the representation after \emph{k} transformer blocks, so
models can be compared at matched relative depth (\emph{k} divided by
the number of blocks). As untrained controls, we built each architecture
with random initial weights (the library's default initialisation, seed
0): a \texttt{LlamaForCausalLM} from MaxToki-217M's configuration with
the same vocabulary, scGPT's \texttt{TransformerModel} with the
whole-human configuration, and STATE's model with all weights random
except its gene table. STATE never trains this table (fixed ESM2 protein
embeddings), so it is the same in the trained and untrained models; the
learned projection that reads it is random.

\subsection{Datasets and annotations}\label{datasets-and-annotations}

The main data are the Tabula Sapiens (Tabula Sapiens Consortium 2022)
immune, kidney and lung datasets from CZ CELLxGENE Discover ({CZI Cell
Science Program et al.} 2025; Tabula Sapiens Consortium, n.d.-a, n.d.-b,
n.d.-c). For the immune dataset we used a 20,000-cell subset; its cell
identifiers are listed in the code repository. These files hold log(1 +
counts per 10,000), written log1p below, in their main matrix, computed
from counts corrected for ambient RNA, and the original integer counts
in their raw matrix. All model inputs, the expression-only
representations and the co-expression matrix were computed from the raw
counts, so they include ambient RNA. Most selected cells come from 10x
3' assays; about 4\% are Smart-seq2 cells, whose counts are read counts.
Contexts are cell types, with cells pooled over the three datasets: the
12 cell types with the most cells (macrophage, pulmonary alveolar type 2
cell, kidney epithelial cell, capillary endothelial cell, CD4-positive
alpha-beta T cell, CD8-positive alpha-beta T cell, B cell, basal cell,
pulmonary alveolar type 1 cell, intermediate monocyte, neutrophil and
classical monocyte), each with at least 1000 cells. We took 1000
randomly chosen cells per cell type (seed 0). Some cell types come from
one dataset and others mix two or three (for example, most macrophages
are lung cells), so a shift between two cell types can also reflect a
different tissue mix.

For independent replication we used the human CD34+ bone-marrow data of
Setty et al. (Setty et al. 2019) (5780 cells) as distributed with scVelo
(Bergen et al. 2020; {scVelo project}, n.d.), which holds velocyto
spliced and unspliced integer counts for 14,319 pre-filtered genes
(11,842 in the MaxToki vocabulary); its main matrix holds the spliced
counts only. Because most Tabula Sapiens counts come from 10x assays
that also count intronic reads, the main Setty analysis ranks genes by
the sum of spliced and unspliced counts; a robustness run used spliced
counts only. Contexts are the nine differentiation clusters with at
least 130 cells: two haematopoietic stem cell clusters, precursors,
common lymphoid progenitors, two erythroid clusters, two monocyte
clusters and dendritic cells. Megakaryocytes (61 cells) were excluded,
and all 5719 cells of the nine kept clusters were used.

Gene Ontology (GO) annotations ({Ashburner et al.} 2000; {Gene Ontology
Consortium et al.} 2023) were taken from the gene-to-GO file distributed
with GEARS (Roohani et al. 2024, 2022). A gene belongs to a functional
pole only if it is annotated directly to one of the listed GO terms;
annotations were not propagated through the GO hierarchy. Curated
transcription-factor (TF) targets were all human TRRUST v2 interactions
({Han et al.} 2018b, 2018a) together with DoRothEA interactions of
confidence A or B (Garcia-Alonso et al. 2019; Saez-Rodriguez Lab, n.d.).
Gene symbols were mapped to Ensembl identifiers with the Geneformer
dictionaries (version gc104M) (Theodoris et al. 2023; Theodoris, n.d.).

\subsection{Input encoding and extraction of contextual gene
representations}\label{input-encoding-and-extraction-of-contextual-gene-representations}

MaxToki uses Geneformer's rank-value encoding (Theodoris et al. 2023): a
cell's counts are normalised to the cell total, each gene is divided by
its median from Geneformer's gc104M gene-median dictionary (MaxToki's
vocabulary holds the same 20,271 genes; the released MaxToki checkpoints
include no median dictionary of their own), and the non-zero genes are
ranked in decreasing order. A per-cell scale factor does not change the
order within a cell, so the order is that of raw count divided by gene
median. The first 1022 genes were kept and wrapped in the beginning- and
end-of-sequence tokens (1024 tokens at most), a quarter of the model's
4096-token limit, to limit compute. Most cells express more genes than
this, so MaxToki saw roughly the top 40\% of a typical cell's genes,
ranked by count divided by gene median. On real cells, this ordering
gives the same token sequences as a separate rank-value tokenizer we
wrote earlier for MaxToki, except that genes whose count-to-median
values are equal after rounding to 32-bit floating point can appear in
swapped order; we did not compare against an official MaxToki tokenizer.

We recorded, for each gene in a panel, its hidden state at that gene's
token position and averaged it over the cells of one cell type. The main
MaxToki-217M extraction used layers 0, 4, 8 and 11. A separate
extraction of all 12 hidden states (the embedding output and all 11
transformer layers) with a 4000-gene panel, on the same cells, gave the
full depth profile. Four design choices guard against known sources of
false signal. (1) Pairs of cell types, not only genes found in every
cell type: each per-gene mean is built from at most 50 occurrences in
each half of the cells (see (3)), and we call a gene count-balanced in a
cell type if it reaches that cap in both halves. In the main extraction
only 137 genes are count-balanced in all 12 cell types, many of them
broadly expressed housekeeping genes (the single-cell analogue of the
``stopwords'' that are most context-sensitive in language models
(Ethayarajh 2019)), whereas each pair of cell types shares a median of
1391 count-balanced genes. So we compare cell types two at a time. The
panel contains genes with at least 25 occurrences in at least two cell
types (the 6000 most frequent such genes). (2) Cell-level split halves:
the cells of each cell type were split into two equal halves (500 cells
each in the main extraction, 300 each in the 600-cell runs). So every
per-gene mean is estimated twice, from different cells, and a result
counts only if it repeats in both halves. (3) An occurrence cap: a
per-gene mean has a standard error proportional to 1/√\emph{n}, and
unequal counts inject context-dependent noise into any interaction term,
so at most 50 occurrences were accumulated per (gene, cell type,
partition), over a shuffled cell order. A (gene, cell type) entry is
used only if it is count-balanced, that is, it reached the cap in both
partitions. (4) Per-dimension z-scoring: before EXCESS, the functional
axes and every other cosine-based measure, every hidden dimension was
standardised over all count-balanced entries, to remove the inflation by
a few dominant (``rogue'') dimensions that a scalar anisotropy
correction misses ({Timkey and van Schijndel} 2021). The anisotropy
check (below) also reports cosines on the raw representations, for
comparison.

Other extractions used the same procedure with these settings. Scaling
pair: MaxToki-217M and MaxToki-1B on the same 600 cells per cell type
with a 5000-gene panel (cap 50), at layers 2 and 4 of MaxToki-217M and
layers 4 and 7 of MaxToki-1B, so that the models can be compared at
matched relative depth (layer 4 of 11 = 0.36 versus layer 7 of 20 =
0.35; layer 2 of 11 = 0.18 versus layer 4 of 20 = 0.20). Random-weights
control: the same cells and the same 5000-gene panel as the MaxToki-217M
scaling run, at layer 4. Setty data: cap 25, a floor of 15 occurrences
in at least two clusters, a 5000-gene panel, layers 2 and 4.

scGPT and STATE were run on exactly the same cells, cell types and
partitions as the 600-cell MaxToki runs, with the same 5000-gene panel
and cap 50, each with its own standard input. scGPT (following its
checkpoint configuration and its cell-embedding pipeline): the raw
counts of all expressed genes in scGPT's vocabulary were quantile-binned
into 50 bins within the cell, 1199 of them were then sampled at random
if the cell had more, and the \texttt{\textless{}cls\textgreater{}}
token was prepended; layers 2, 4 and 9 (relative depth 0.17, 0.33 and
0.75). STATE (through its own data collator): raw counts over the genes
in its protein table, the top 2047 expressed genes by count; we kept
only positions holding expressed genes; layers 3, 6 and 12 (relative
depth 0.19, 0.38 and 0.75). Of the 5000 panel genes, 4964 are in scGPT's
vocabulary and 4969 in STATE's protein table. Each model's own
count-balanced entries were used. The untrained controls used the same
cells and panel.

\subsection{Expression-only
representations}\label{expression-only-representations}

To test directly whether expression alone produces the functional
organisation, we built contextual gene representations with no model,
from the same cells, cell types and partitions as the 600-cell runs.
Landmark version: in each cell type and partition, gene \emph{g} was
represented by the vector of Pearson correlations, across those 300
cells, between \emph{g}'s log1p(counts per 10,000) and that of each of
512 landmark genes (the most variable panel genes over all selected
cells). Principal-component version: the same, with the scores of 256
principal components of the z-scored expression of all selected cells in
place of the landmarks. Both are correlation profiles within one cell
type, so they cannot see genes that rise and fall together between cell
types. Our co-expression measure (next sections) pools all cell types
and so includes that part. We therefore added a third version, built on
axes from all cell types pooled, that we expected to respond to that
part. Each of its vectors is still an average over cells of one cell
type, divided by the gene's total expression there, so the gene's level
in each cell type cancels out; it may therefore not capture
co-expression between cell types either, and we did not check this
directly. Centroid version: gene \emph{g} in a cell type and partition
was placed at the expression-weighted mean position of its expressing
cells in the first 50 principal components of the same pooled, z-scored
expression, that is, where in the pooled cell space the gene is
expressed within that cell type. As in the model extractions, the cells
of each cell type and partition were put in a random order and only the
first 50 cells expressing the gene were used, so each vector averages as
many cells as a model vector averages occurrences. Without this cap a
vector averages all expressing cells (a median of 202 per entry; 10th to
90th percentile 108 to 277); we report that version only as a check. We
first built the centroid version with 512 components and no cap. It
failed the positive control: the shift shared by all genes did not
replicate between the two halves of the cells (cosine −0.22), because
z-scoring each dimension gives the many noisy high-numbered components
equal weight. With 50 components, a common default in single-cell
analysis, it passes (replication +0.79); we did not run other numbers of
components through the pipeline. The 512-component and all-cells results
are in Additional file 1. The landmark and principal-component versions
are correlations across all 300 cells of each half, so they are not
matched to the models' 50 occurrences. All three used the same gene
panel and the same count-balanced entries as MaxToki-217M and went
through the identical pipeline (EXCESS, functional axes, all nulls).
``Layer'' does not apply to them.

\subsection{Two-way decomposition and contextualisation strength
(EXCESS)}\label{two-way-decomposition-and-contextualisation-strength-excess}

For gene \emph{g} in cell type \emph{c}, we write \emph{v(g,c) = μ +
a(g) + b(c) + E(g,c)}: a gene main effect \emph{a(g)} (the gene's
identity), a context main effect \emph{b(c)} (a shift shared by all
genes in that cell type, expected because attention pools over the
cell), and a gene-specific interaction \emph{E(g,c)}, the only term
where a gene-specific response to context can appear.

For a cell-type pair (\emph{c}\textsubscript{0},
\emph{c}\textsubscript{1}), the relative shift of gene \emph{g} is
δ(\emph{g}) = {[}\emph{v}(\emph{g},\emph{c}\textsubscript{1}) −
\emph{v}(\emph{g},\emph{c}\textsubscript{0}){]} −
mean\textsubscript{\emph{g′}}{[}\emph{v}(\emph{g′},\emph{c}\textsubscript{1})
− \emph{v}(\emph{g′},\emph{c}\textsubscript{0}){]}: the gene's own shift
minus the average shift of all genes. We computed δ separately in the
two cell partitions (δ\textsubscript{0}, δ\textsubscript{1}) and defined
EXCESS = mean cos(δ\textsubscript{0}(\emph{g}),
δ\textsubscript{1}(\emph{g})) − mean cos(δ\textsubscript{0}(\emph{g}),
δ\textsubscript{1}(perm \emph{g})): the same gene's shift agreeing
across independent cells, minus a gene-shuffled control. EXCESS = 0
means no gene-specific response to context. The means are pooled over
all (gene, pair) entries from the cell-type pairs with at least 200
count-balanced genes (150 in the cross-model comparison of
contextualisation strength and in the Setty analysis), which equals a
mean over pairs weighted by their number of genes. In the 600-cell data
three of the 66 pairs have 150 to 199 such genes, so EXCESS in the
cross-model comparison can differ by up to 0.003 from EXCESS in the
functional-organisation controls. 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) come
from 2000 bootstrap resamples of genes, in which each resampled gene
brings all of its pair entries. As a positive control we report the
replication of the context main effect across partitions (the cosine
between the two partitions' mean shifts).

\textbf{Anisotropy.} On the raw (unstandardised) representations we
computed the mean cosine of random different-gene pairs (the anisotropy
baseline (Ethayarajh 2019)), same-gene self-similarity across cell
types, and the share of variance on the first principal component (PC),
and repeated these after per-dimension z-scoring. Self-similarity was
averaged over all genes count-balanced in at least two cell types (5433
at layer 4). The anisotropy baseline is the mean over 6000 random pairs
of (gene, cell type) entries, and the first-PC share comes from 4000
randomly drawn entries.

\textbf{Rank-position control.} MaxToki encodes token position, and a
gene's rank changes between cell types. We computed each gene's mean
rank in each cell type over the cells in which it was among the 1022
genes given to MaxToki (only these cells give the gene a
representation), using the same cells as the extraction. We split genes
at the median absolute rank change into rank-stable and rank-moving
halves and recomputed EXCESS in each, correlated the size of the shift
with the rank change, and recomputed EXCESS after a linear regression of
the shift on the gene's mean rank in each of the two cell types and on
its absolute rank change. Because rank could act in a curved way, we
repeated the regression with squared, product and log terms of the
ranks. As a second check, we replaced the gene-shuffled control with a
rank-matched one: each gene's shift was compared with that of the gene
nearest to it in (rank in one cell type, rank in the other). If rank
drove the shift, genes with the same ranks would share it.

\subsection{Functional axes and reproducible interaction
power}\label{functional-axes-and-reproducible-interaction-power}

A functional axis \emph{u} is the unit vector separating two GO-defined
gene classes in the gene main effect, \emph{u} ∝ mean(\emph{a}(\emph{g})
\textbar{} class A) − mean(\emph{a}(\emph{g}) \textbar{} class B), where
\emph{a}(\emph{g}) is the gene's z-scored representation averaged over
partitions and count-balanced cell types. Genes annotated to both poles
were removed from both. The axes were nuclear versus surface/secreted
(nucleus GO:0005634, chromatin GO:0000785 and DNA binding GO:0003677,
versus plasma membrane GO:0005886, extracellular region GO:0005576 and
extracellular space GO:0005615); mitochondrion (GO:0005739) versus
cytoskeleton (GO:0005856); transcription (regulation of DNA-templated
transcription GO:0006355 and DNA-binding transcription factor activity
GO:0003700) versus transport and signalling (ion transport GO:0006811
and signalling receptor activity GO:0038023); and cell cycle
(GO:0007049) versus cell differentiation (GO:0030154). Axis validity is
the five-fold cross-validated area under the receiver operating
characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) with which ⟨\emph{a}(\emph{g}),
\emph{u}⟩ separates held-out pole genes.

Reproducible interaction power measures how much context moves genes
along \emph{u} in a way that replicates across independent cells. For
genes count-balanced in at least 9 cell types, we projected each
partition's representation onto \emph{u},
\emph{q}\textsubscript{\emph{p}}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) =
⟨\emph{v}\textsubscript{\emph{p}}(\emph{g},\emph{c}), \emph{u}⟩;
regressed out the gene's mean rank in that cell type (linear and
quadratic terms; ``rank-controlled''); took the two-way interaction
\emph{I}\textsubscript{\emph{p}}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) (the projection
minus its gene mean and context mean, plus the grand mean); and averaged
\emph{I}\textsubscript{0}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) ·
\emph{I}\textsubscript{1}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) over all (gene, cell type)
entries. Noise averages to zero in this product, so only modulation that
reproduces across the two partitions survives. Functional-z is the
z-score of this power against 300 axes built from random gene partitions
of the same pole sizes. We report it at layers 4, 8 and 11, without and
with the rank regression. A cell type's loading on an axis is the mean
projection onto \emph{u} of the z-scored layer-4 representations
(averaged over partitions) of the genes count-balanced in that cell
type. These gene sets differ between cell types and the loadings are not
rank-controlled, so we use them only to describe the ordering of cell
types.

\subsection{Null models for functional
organisation}\label{null-models-for-functional-organisation}

Functional gene sets may be co-expressed, and co-expressed genes co-vary
across cells whatever the model ``understands''. We therefore compared
each of the three axes with well-separated poles (nuclear/surface,
mitochondrion/cytoskeleton and transcription/transport; the
cell-cycle/differentiation axis separates its poles only weakly and was
not tested) with null axes whose two poles have the same sizes as the
functional poles, using the rank-controlled power. Null 1 (random axes)
is the null behind functional-z and was computed at layers 4, 8 and 11;
its empirical p was computed at layer 4. Nulls 2 and 3 were computed at
layer 4, and null 4 at layers 4 and 8. In the 600-cell comparison each
model was tested at its own depth-matched layer. Co-expression was the
Pearson correlation of log1p(counts per 10,000), computed from the raw
counts over 3000 cells from each of the three Tabula Sapiens datasets,
and the coherence of a gene set was its mean pairwise correlation.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Random axes:} random genes with the functional poles' sizes
  (300 axes). For functional-z the two poles do not overlap. For the
  empirical p at layer 4 we drew a separate set of 300 random axes, with
  the two poles chosen separately so that they can share genes.
\item
  \textbf{Strongly co-expressed modules:} each null pole was the set of
  genes most correlated with a random seed gene (300 axes). These
  modules are much more co-expressed than the functional poles
  (Results), so this is a strong null rather than a matched one.
\item
  \textbf{Size- and co-expression-matched sets:} each null pole was
  built from the \emph{k} genes most correlated with a random seed gene
  plus randomly chosen genes, with \emph{k} chosen by bisection so that
  the pole's coherence was within 10\% of the functional pole's (300
  axes). We also kept pole size fixed and traced power against the share
  of each pole drawn from the genes most co-expressed with the seed gene
  (0 to 100\%, 25 draws per step).
\item
  \textbf{Size- and tightness-matched sets:} the same construction with
  representation tightness (the mean pairwise cosine of the pole genes'
  main effects \emph{a}(\emph{g})) in place of co-expression (300 axes).
  When a functional pole was less tight than random genes, so that even
  a random set was at least as tight, a random set of the same size was
  used for that pole. This happened in at most 11 of 300 null axes for
  any axis in the four trained models. In the landmark expression-only
  version it happened in at most 36 of 300. In the centroid
  expression-only version it happened in at most 3 of 300. The other
  pole of each axis was always matched. Such sets make the null harder,
  not easier, to beat.
\end{enumerate}

For each null, the empirical one-sided p is (\emph{k} + 1)/(\emph{N} +
1), where \emph{k} is the number of the \emph{N} = 300 null axes with
power at least as large as the functional axis; when no null axis
reached it, p takes its smallest possible value, 1/301. Empirical p
values were used because the null power distributions are right-skewed.

\subsection{Causal steering test}\label{causal-steering-test}

To test whether the model uses the functional direction, we added it to
the residual stream at half of a cell's gene positions and read the
model's own next-gene output logits at the other half. The direction was
the raw (unstandardised) difference between the mean layer-4
representations of the nuclear-pole and surface-pole genes (poles made
disjoint, as for the axes). It was added at the layer-4 representation
(the output of the fourth transformer block). For each of 30 immune
cells (at most 512 tokens), gene positions were randomly split into a
steered half and a read half, so any effect at the read positions must
travel through attention. The readout used a wider gene set than the
direction: every gene token in MaxToki's vocabulary annotated to a pole
(4844 nuclear and 5520 surface tokens; genes annotated to both removed).
The readout (specificity) was the mean change in logit over the
nuclear-pole tokens minus that over the surface-pole tokens. Strengths
were 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 times the mean residual-stream norm at that layer
in one reference cell; we report 0.5 as the headline. Pushing toward
+\emph{u} and toward −\emph{u} and taking the difference (the signed
swing) cancels any effect that does not depend on the sign of the push.
The first control was a random direction of the same norm, drawn fresh
for each cell; significance was a one-sided sign test of functional
swing greater than random swing across cells. This tests whether the
push has a consistent effect in the expected direction. Because a random
direction is not built like the axis, a second control was matched to
the axis: 30 fixed random splits of the pooled nuclear-pole and
surface-pole genes into two groups of the pole sizes, each turned into a
direction exactly like the functional axis and applied to the same
cells, positions and strength (0.5). The orientation of a random split
is arbitrary, so we compared sizes: p = (1 + number of splits whose
absolute mean swing is at least the absolute functional swing) / (30 +
1), whose smallest possible value is 1/(30 + 1) = 0.032. We read the
direction from the sign of the functional swing. With 30 splits these p
values cannot survive a correction for several tests, so we report them
uncorrected, together with \emph{z}, the number of standard deviations
by which the functional swing exceeds the absolute split swings. The
test was repeated at the layer-8 representation and for the
mitochondrion/cytoskeleton and transcription/transport axes at layer 4.
To put the push sizes in context, we measured how far context naturally
moves genes along the same direction: the spread of each gene's
projection onto \emph{u} across its count-balanced cell types.

To compare MaxToki-217M with MaxToki-1B, we repeated the steering test
on the 600-cell extractions at matched relative depth (MaxToki-217M
layer 4, MaxToki-1B layer 7), with a different push unit. MaxToki-1B has
two hidden dimensions that are very large in every gene's
representation. Its raw residual norm is 3.0 times the norm of the part
that differs between genes, against 1.1 times in MaxToki-217M, so a push
scaled to the raw norm would be much stronger in MaxToki-1B. In this
comparison the push unit was therefore the mean norm of the residual
after removing its mean over the cell's gene positions (averaged over 5
cells). We ran all three axes in both sizes, with both controls, and
repeated the nuclear/surface test in both sizes with the axis, and the
split directions, set to zero in the two largest dimensions. The
1000-cell configurations above were also run against the fixed splits.

\subsection{Tests for context-specific gene
function}\label{tests-for-context-specific-gene-function}

\textbf{Directional congruence.} Along each functional axis, we
decomposed each gene's projection into a gene loading \emph{f}(\emph{g})
(mean over contexts), a context loading \emph{h}(\emph{c}) (mean over
genes) and an interaction \emph{e}(\emph{g},\emph{c}). If genes move
toward their own functional pole specifically in cell types that share
that character, \emph{e} should follow \emph{f}·\emph{h}. We estimated
\emph{f} and \emph{h} in one partition and \emph{e} in the other (and
vice versa), measured β = corr(\emph{e}, \emph{f}·\emph{h}) on
rank-residualised projections, and compared β with 300 null axes whose
poles were matched to the functional poles on size and co-expression (as
in null 3 above).

\textbf{Placement near curated targets.} For TFs count-balanced in at
least 6 cell types with at least 15 curated targets in the gene panel,
we asked (A) whether, in each cell type, the TF's representation (with
the context mean removed) is closer by cosine to the centroid of its
curated targets than to the centroid of non-targets matched on
co-expression with the TF (200 redraws), and (B) whether this excess
closeness rises in cell types where the targets are more highly
expressed (Spearman correlation across cell types with target activity,
the negative of the targets' mean rank). The matched non-targets are
matched on co-expression with the TF but not on gene type, and curated
target lists are rich in regulators, so (C) we also asked whether the TF
is closer to its own targets than to the targets of the other TFs tested
(same cell type, same measure). A cell type was used for a TF only if at
least 8 of its targets were count-balanced there, and a TF needed at
least 3 such cell types. All three questions were tested across TFs with
sign tests.

\textbf{Lineage transcription factors.} We also tried a test on 14 TFs
with lineage-specific roles in immune cells (SPI1, IRF4, IRF8, GATA3,
MAFB, BCL6, PRDM1, TBX21, RUNX1, RUNX3, BATF, MAF, BACH2 and ZEB2)
(Heinz et al. 2010). It asked whether these TFs move more between cell
types than other genes do. A gene's movement was the part of its
gene-specific shift in each cell type that repeats in both cell halves
(the product of the two halves' shifts, averaged over cell types;
MaxToki-217M, 1000-cell extraction, layers 4 and 8). The controls were
genes matched on abundance, and genes matched on abundance and on how
much their co-expression partners change between cell types; p values
came from a permutation that keeps TF families together. Because
movement is averaged over cell types, the test needs genes
count-balanced in at least 9 of the 12 cell types (Results).

\subsection{Cross-model, scaling and random-weights
comparison}\label{cross-model-scaling-and-random-weights-comparison}

All 600-cell representations (MaxToki-217M, MaxToki-1B, scGPT, STATE,
the three untrained models and the three expression-only
representations) share the cells, cell types, partitions and gene panel.
Model vectors and centroid vectors average 50 occurrences or expressing
cells per entry; the landmark and principal-component versions use all
300 cells of each half. EXCESS (on context pairs with at least 150
count-balanced genes) and nuclear/surface functional-z were computed the
same way for every representation. Each trained model was scored at two
depths (MaxToki-217M layers 2 and 4; MaxToki-1B layers 4 and 7; scGPT
layers 2 and 4; STATE layers 3 and 6) and each untrained model at its
depth-matched layer. In this comparison functional-z was computed
without the rank regression, on genes count-balanced in at least 6 of
the 12 cell types, against 1000 random axes whose two poles do not
overlap. In the Setty replication it used genes count-balanced in at
least 4 of the 9 clusters and 200 random axes, whose two poles were
drawn separately, so they could share genes. Each model has its own
count-balanced entries, so we repeated the comparison using only the
(cell type, gene) entries that are count-balanced in every
representation. The rank regression, all four nulls and both Level 2
tests were also run on the four trained models at their depth-matched
layers (MaxToki-217M layer 4, MaxToki-1B layer 7, scGPT layer 4, STATE
layer 6) and on the three expression-only representations. For the
untrained models we also ran the curated-target test, and for untrained
scGPT and STATE the rank regression on the functional axes. On the
untrained models only random axes (null 1) were used: we ran none of the
co-expression and tightness nulls (nulls 2 to 4) and not the
directional-congruence test on them. We built no untrained MaxToki-1B.
With 600 cells per cell type fewer genes reach the cap in many cell
types, so these runs used genes count-balanced in at least 6 cell types.
For the functional axes and the co-expression nulls, at least 9 cell
types gave the same conclusions (Additional file 1); the Level 2 tests
were run only with at least 6. For the rank regression of non-MaxToki
representations we used MaxToki's mean rank on the same cells as an
abundance covariate. For the functional axes and the two co-expression
nulls we repeated it with the gene's mean log1p(counts per 10,000)
instead.

\subsection{Link to next-gene
prediction}\label{link-to-next-gene-prediction}

For 350 Tabula Sapiens immune cells with at least 60 expressed
vocabulary genes (at most 512 tokens), and up to 20 target positions per
cell from the sixth gene onward, we compared the model's log-probability
of the true gene given the cell's own preceding genes with that given
the same number of preceding genes from another cell (a chimeric
context). The donor was another cell, chosen at random, with at least as
many genes as the real prefix. The donor genes used could not include
the target gene, so the chimeric context never contains it. The per-gene
context benefit (log-probability difference, averaged over occurrences;
genes with at least 3 occurrences) was correlated with the gene's
context movement: the mean, over the cell types where the gene is
count-balanced (at least 4), of ‖\emph{z}(\emph{g},\emph{c}) −
mean\textsubscript{\emph{c}} \emph{z}(\emph{g},\emph{c})‖ in the
z-scored layer-4 space. This is one number per gene, unlike EXCESS. We
used a partial Spearman correlation that controls for the log of the
number of times the gene was sampled as a target, with a 95\% CI from
2000 bootstrap resamples of genes.

To compare MaxToki-217M and MaxToki-1B, we ran a paired version on the
same 350 cells, with each gene's context movement taken from the
600-cell extractions (MaxToki-217M layer 4, MaxToki-1B layer 7). The
cells, target positions and donor cells were drawn from seeds that do
not depend on the model, so both models were scored on identical (cell,
position, donor) triples. Because MaxToki is causal, the
log-probabilities for every prefix of a cell come from one forward pass
over the whole cell; on 20 random queries per model this matched
separate prefix passes to within 1.2 × 10\textsuperscript{−5} nats
(largest difference). A second donor arm drew the donor from the same
cell type, which asks whether context helps beyond cell identity.

\subsection{Statistics and
reproducibility}\label{statistics-and-reproducibility}

All random draws use fixed seeds (base seed 0), so every script draws
the same cells, genes and null sets each time it runs. Most analyses
work from saved hidden states; the steering and prediction-link tests
run the model again. Empirical p values are one-sided unless stated. We
corrected for multiple tests in three families. Steering (1000-cell
MaxToki-217M): at each strength separately, the random-direction sign
tests of the four configurations (nuclear/surface at layers 4 and 8;
mitochondrion/cytoskeleton and transcription/transport at layer 4), with
Bonferroni; the three strengths are not corrected against each other.
Curated targets (1000-cell MaxToki-217M): static closeness and its rise
with target activity, at layers 4 and 8, with Bonferroni. Directional
congruence: three axes at layers 4 and 8 in the 1000-cell extraction,
with Holm, counting only positive β; the same Holm correction was
applied over each 600-cell representation's own tests (Table 4). The
fixed-split p values are not corrected (see Causal steering test). All
other p values are not corrected, including the null p values (Table 3),
the own-versus-other-targets test, the curated-target tests on the
600-cell representations (Table 4) and the 600-cell steering tests.
Where Results gives a corrected value for one of these, it names the
correction. Each analysis has its own script: input encoding

\section{Results}\label{results}

The first sections use the main MaxToki-217M extraction (1000 cells per
cell type) unless a model is named; later sections compare all models on
the same 600 cells per cell type. For Level 1, we show that
contextualisation is real, is not explained by rank position and
replicates on a second dataset, and that the nuclear/surface direction
is read by the model when pushed, although not every functional
direction is. Two later sections show that contextualisation is partly
learned and weakly linked to prediction. For Level 2, we compare the
functional organisation with co-expression nulls and with
representations built from expression alone, and run two direct tests
for context-specific gene function.

\subsection{Genes are contextualised gene-specifically; in MaxToki-217M
most reproducibly in the first
layers}\label{genes-are-contextualised-gene-specifically-in-maxtoki-217m-most-reproducibly-in-the-first-layers}

The gene-specific context response is large and highly reproducible
(Table 1, Fig. 1). At layer 4 of the main extraction, EXCESS is +0.758:
the same gene's relative shift agrees at cosine +0.759 across
independent cells, while a different gene's agrees at +0.001, so the
effect is gene-specific. The context main effect replicates at +0.996,
which shows that the measurement works. Layer 0 (the context-free
embedding output) gives exactly 0.000, as expected: a gene has the same
embedding in every cell, so it has no shift to measure. The control that
tests the statistic itself is the different-gene agreement (+0.001). The
full depth scan shows where the response arises: it is highest at layer
1 (+0.847) and layer 2 (+0.835) and then falls to +0.651 at layer 11
(Fig. 1). The gene-specific shift is therefore already present after the
first transformer layer and is most reproducible at layers 1 and 2.
EXCESS is a cosine, so it measures how reproducible the direction of the
shift is, not how far a gene moves. Deeper layers keep most of it (90\%
of the peak at layer 4 and 84\% at layer 8), while the gene-specific
shift becomes somewhat less reproducible toward the output.

EXCESS is the agreement of the same gene's relative context shift across
two independent halves of the cells, minus the agreement for a different
gene, pooled over 66 cell-type pairs (a median of 1391 count-balanced
genes per pair). Gene-level bootstrap 95\% CIs are within ±0.003. The
full depth profile is shown in Fig. 1.

\textbf{Metric validity (anisotropy).} These representations are
anisotropic, as expected (Ethayarajh 2019): the mean cosine of random
different-gene pairs is +0.20 to +0.62 at layers 4--11 (raw). After
per-dimension z-scoring, which brings anisotropy to about 0.00,
same-gene self-similarity across cell types falls with depth: +0.88,
+0.65 and +0.61 at layers 4, 8 and 11 (raw, uncorrected: +0.92, +0.85
and +0.69). So genes move more between cell types in upper layers, as
Ethayarajh found in language models. Simply subtracting the raw
anisotropy baseline gives +0.62, +0.24 and +0.48 instead. This series
dips at layer 8, where raw anisotropy peaks, so the layer-8 value mostly
reflects the baseline. We z-score each dimension before every
cosine-based measure (Methods), because a few dominant dimensions can
drive raw cosines and one subtraction does not remove them ({Timkey and
van Schijndel} 2021). With all three versions, self-similarity is higher
at layer 4 than at layer 11. Together with the EXCESS profile, this
means that upper layers move genes more but less reproducibly. After
z-scoring, the first principal component carries only 0.07 of the
variance at layer 11, so no single direction dominates the cosines.

\subsection{The effect is not explained by the gene's rank
position}\label{the-effect-is-not-explained-by-the-genes-rank-position}

MaxToki reads a cell as a rank-ordered gene list and encodes token
position, so a gene ranking 5th in one cell type and 500th in another is
an obvious confound. Three checks show that rank does not drive the
gene-specific shift (Fig. 2). At layer 4 the response is about the same
for genes whose rank changes less than the median (+0.749; median change
27 positions) and more than the median (+0.767; 107 positions). 99\% of
it survives a linear regression on the gene's rank in each cell type and
its rank change (+0.749), and 99\% survives a regression that also
allows curved effects of rank (+0.747); at layers 8 and 11, 98\% and
97\% survive the linear regression. And a gene's shift agrees only a
little with that of the gene closest to it in rank in both cell types
(+0.058, against +0.759 for the same gene and +0.001 for a random gene).
Measured against this rank-matched gene instead of a random one, EXCESS
is still +0.701 at layer 4 (93\% of the full value), and 90\% and 88\%
at layers 8 and 11. So genes with the same ranks share only a small part
of the shift. This part is an upper bound on what rank explains, because
genes with the same ranks also tend to have similar expression levels.
The size of a gene's shift correlates only weakly with its rank change
(ρ = 0.26 at layer 4 and 0.31 at layer 11). These checks cannot exclude
every way in which position could enter a causal model.

\subsection{The effect replicates on an independent
dataset}\label{the-effect-replicates-on-an-independent-dataset}

In MaxToki-217M, on the Setty CD34+ bone-marrow data, with nine
differentiation clusters rather than mature cell types as contexts, the
phenomenon replicates: EXCESS is +0.713 at layer 2 and +0.713 at layer 4
(gene-shuffled null +0.0036 and +0.0039), and functional-z (without the
rank regression, against random axes only; Methods) is +5.0 and +5.0 for
the nuclear/surface axis and +16.1 and +8.8 for the
mitochondrion/cytoskeleton axis. We did not run the rank control, the
co-expression nulls or the Level 2 tests on these data. Using spliced
counts only gives the same picture (EXCESS +0.702 and nuclear/surface
functional-z +9.3 at layer 4). Neither the contextualisation nor its
organisation along these two axes is specific to Tabula Sapiens or to
mature cell types.

\subsection{Expression alone gives a gene-specific change, but not its
organisation}\label{expression-alone-gives-a-gene-specific-change-but-not-its-organisation}

The nulls above ask whether functional gene sets are special among gene
sets with the same co-expression, inside the model. A different question
is whether a representation built only from expression shows the same
contextualisation and organisation. On the same 600 cells per cell type,
we compared MaxToki-217M (layer 4) with three expression-only
representations (Methods). The landmark and principal-component versions
use correlations within one cell type; the centroid version places each
gene where it is expressed in a cell space built from all cells pooled.
All three change with cell type in a gene-specific way (EXCESS +0.427,
+0.218 and +0.321, against +0.738 for MaxToki-217M on the same cells),
and 99\% of the centroid version's change survives the rank regression.
So a reproducible, gene-specific change between cell types does not need
a model: part of it follows from where each gene is expressed within
each cell type. EXCESS depends on how many cells each vector averages.
With all expressing cells instead of 50, the centroid version reaches
+0.605, and the two correlation versions use all 300 cells of each half.
These values should therefore not be ranked against the models'
(Methods). But none of the three is clearly organised along the
functional axes. Rank-controlled functional-z is −1.4, +1.8 and +1.4
(landmarks), +0.6, +0.4 and +0.8 (principal components) and −0.3, −2.1
and +0.5 (centroids), against +16.7, +7.5 and +5.0 in MaxToki-217M
(Table 3).

The difference also shows in how co-expressed gene sets behave. We
express each null's power as a multiple of the power of random poles in
the same representation. In MaxToki-217M, sets matched on co-expression
reach 1.8--6.0 times random, modules of strongly co-expressed genes
5.6--10.1 times, and poles made only of genes co-expressed with a seed
gene 6.1--9.8 times. The other models behave the same way: strong
modules reach 5.0--8.9 times random in MaxToki-1B, 3.3--4.2 times in
scGPT and 12.5--18.0 times in STATE. The expression-only versions do
not. The two within-cell-type versions barely respond to co-expression.
Matched sets reach 1.4--1.6 times random with landmarks and 1.3--1.4
times with principal components, and strong modules about the same
(1.4--1.5 and 1.5 times). In the centroid version, co-expressed sets do
no better than random sets: strong modules reach 0.9--1.0 times random,
and poles made only of co-expressed genes 0.9 times. So in every model,
context moves genes much more along directions that separate
co-expressed gene sets than along random directions, and in none of the
three expression-only versions does it. This holds for co-expressed sets
in general, not for functional sets in particular. It does not show that
the models add information beyond co-expression: other expression-only
constructions might come closer (Limitations).

\subsection{The nuclear/surface direction is read by the model, but not
every functional direction
is}\label{the-nuclearsurface-direction-is-read-by-the-model-but-not-every-functional-direction-is}

In the steering test (Fig. 4), adding the nuclear/surface direction to
half of a cell's genes moves the model's own logits at the untouched
half with the sign of the push. Nuclear-gene logits rise relative to
surface-gene logits for a push toward the nuclear pole (specificity
+0.085) and fall relative to them for a push toward the surface pole
(−0.097), a signed swing of +0.182. A random direction of the same norm,
drawn fresh for each cell, gives +0.001 on average, near zero by design.
The swing is +0.079, +0.182, +0.184 at strengths 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0, so
it stops growing above 0.5. At 0.5 it exceeds the random swing in 22 of
30 cells (p = 0.0081; Bonferroni-adjusted 0.032); at 1.0 in 20 of 30
cells (p = 0.049; Bonferroni-adjusted 0.20). The effect is stronger at
the layer-8 representation (swing +0.232, 30 of 30 cells, p = 9.3 ×
10\textsuperscript{−10}). Against 30 fixed random splits of the same
pole genes, built like the axis, the layer-4 swing is larger than 30 of
30 of them (0 of 30 at least as large; p = 1/31, \emph{z} = +2.6) and
the layer-8 swing larger than 30 of 30 (0 of 30; p = 1/31, \emph{z} =
+6.1). The direction therefore propagates through attention and changes
the model's predictions, more than directions built from random groups
of the same genes.

This does not generalise to every functional axis. The
mitochondrion/cytoskeleton direction has no significant effect at
strengths 0.25 and 0.5 (swing +0.024 and +0.067; above the random swing
in 17 of 30 and 15 of 30 cells, p = 0.29 and 0.57). At strength 1.0 its
swing is +0.269, above the random swing in 20 of 30 cells (p = 0.049,
Bonferroni-adjusted 0.20). The split control was run only at strength
0.5. There the swing is not distinct from the random splits (18 of 30
splits at least as large; p = 0.613). The strength-1.0 swing was not
compared with the splits. The transcription/transport direction has a
reversed effect that grows with strength (swing −0.139, −0.268, −0.678).
At strength 0.5 the functional swing exceeds the random one in only 2 of
30 cells (two-sided sign test p = 8.7 × 10\textsuperscript{−7}; 2 of 30
at 0.25 and 0 of 30 at 1.0). At 0.5 its size also exceeds that of 30 of
30 random splits, but in the reverse direction. We have no explanation
for the reversal; it shows that an axis that separates genes is not
automatically read in the expected direction. The pushes are also large
compared with natural variation. Across cell types, a gene's projection
on the nuclear/surface direction has a median range of 2.62 (95th
percentile 4.57). The middle push (7.6 units) is about 3 times that
median range, and the largest (15.2 units) about 6 times. The smallest
push (3.8 units) is about 1.4 times the median range, below the
95th-percentile range. It already gives a swing of +0.079, above the
random direction in 23 of 30 cells (p = 0.0026; Bonferroni-adjusted
0.010); it was not tested against the random splits. Even this push
moves half of a cell's genes along the axis at once, which natural
context does not do. The test therefore shows that the model can read
this axis, not that the natural, context-driven changes along it are
used.

The nuclear/surface result holds in MaxToki-1B. On the 600-cell
extractions at matched relative depth, with the push scaled to the part
of the residual that differs between genes, the swing at strength 0.5 is
+0.212 in MaxToki-217M and +0.205 in MaxToki-1B. It exceeds a random
direction in 25 of 30 and 30 of 30 cells (p = 1.6 ×
10\textsuperscript{−4} and 9.3 × 10\textsuperscript{−10}). Against 30
random splits, 0 of 30 and 0 of 30 are at least as large (mean absolute
split swing 0.067 and 0.047; p = 1/31 and 1/31, \emph{z} = +3.6 and
+4.2). In MaxToki-1B, 52\% of the axis lies in the two very large hidden
dimensions. With those coordinates set to zero, the swing is +0.324 (0
of 30 splits at least as large; \emph{z} = +6.3), so they do not carry
the effect. In MaxToki-217M, which has no such very large dimensions,
only 13\% of the axis lies in its own two largest dimensions. Even so,
with them set to zero the swing falls from +0.212 to +0.131 (splits at
least as large: 1 of 30; p = 0.065, \emph{z} = +1.9), so the swing is no
longer clearly above the splits. In this model part of the effect goes
through its two largest dimensions. The other two axes behave
differently in the two sizes. In MaxToki-1B both are read in the
expected direction: the swing is +0.400 for mitochondrion/cytoskeleton
(\emph{z} = +4.7 against the splits) and +0.111 for
transcription/transport (\emph{z} = +4.6). In MaxToki-217M the
transcription/transport direction is again read in reverse (−0.242), and
the mitochondrion/cytoskeleton swing (+0.168) is not distinct from the
random splits (7 of 30 at least as large; p = 0.258). So only the
nuclear/surface direction is read as expected in both sizes and both
extractions; in the larger model all three directions are.

\subsection{No context-specific gene function beyond
co-expression}\label{no-context-specific-gene-function-beyond-co-expression}

Two direct tests for context-appropriate gene function find no
context-specific signal beyond co-expression. (1) Directional congruence
asks whether genes move toward their own functional pole in the cell
types that lean toward that pole (β, Methods). For no axis was β
significantly larger than for null axes matched on size and
co-expression. At layer 4, β is +0.16, +0.10 and +0.23 for the three
axes, all positive but within the upper tail of size- and
co-expression-matched null axes, whose means are near zero (p = 0.116,
0.086 and 0.053; none significant after Holm correction over 6 tests; at
layer 8, β is −0.16, −0.11 and +0.09). So at layer 4 the trend points
the expected way but is not significant; the null spread of β (standard
deviation 0.08--0.13) means the test could detect only clear congruence.
(2) Placement near curated targets: across 26 TFs, at layer 4 a TF's
representation is slightly closer to the centroid of its curated targets
than to that of co-expression-matched non-targets (mean cosine excess
+0.021; 20 of 26 TFs positive; sign test p = 0.005, Bonferroni-adjusted
over 4 tests 0.019). Compared with the targets of the other TFs tested,
a TF is only slightly closer to its own targets (+0.008; 17 of 26 TFs
positive; p = 0.084), so the closeness is not shown to be specific to
each TF's own targets; much of it may reflect where regulators and their
usual targets sit in general. The closeness does not rise significantly
in the cell types where the targets are more active (mean ρ = +0.084; 15
of 26 TFs positive; p = 0.28, Bonferroni-adjusted 1.00); the trend is
positive but weak, and each TF's ρ rests on as few as 3 cell types. At
layer 8 neither effect is significant (static p = 0.423; modulation ρ =
+0.097, p = 0.28). We did not run an untrained model on these cells. On
the 600-cell extraction only 11 TFs qualify (Table 4). There the
closeness is smaller and not significant (+0.011; 8 of 11 TFs positive;
p = 0.113). The untrained MaxToki-217M gives a mean no smaller (+0.030),
but only 6 of 11 TFs are positive (p = 0.500). That 11-TF test has
little power, so we cannot say whether the closeness is learned, or
whether it holds beyond these 26 TFs. A third attempt, on 14 lineage
TFs, is uninformative rather than negative. The test needs a gene to be
count-balanced in at least 9 of the 12 cell types. 10 of the 14 TFs are
in the 6000-gene panel, but only 1 (RUNX1) met that bar. These
regulators are weakly expressed, or are expressed in only a few of the
cell types we used.

\subsection{MaxToki-1B, scGPT and STATE also contextualise genes, but
show no context-specific gene function beyond
co-expression}\label{maxtoki-1b-scgpt-and-state-also-contextualise-genes-but-show-no-context-specific-gene-function-beyond-co-expression}

We ran the full set of controls and both Level 2 tests on every trained
model and expression-only representation of the same 600 cells per cell
type (Table 3, Table 4). Three results hold in every model ((1), (3) and
(4) below), one holds only in some models ((2)), and one holds only in
MaxToki-217M ((5)). (1) Every model contextualises genes strongly and
gene-specifically (EXCESS +0.738, +0.764, +0.813 and +0.867 for
MaxToki-217M, MaxToki-1B, scGPT and STATE), and almost all of it
survives the rank regression (99\%, 99\%, 98\% and 99\%). (2)
Organisation beyond random axes holds only partly. The MaxToki models
organise the change along all three functional axes beyond random axes
(rank-controlled functional-z for the nuclear/surface axis +16.7 and
+8.0), whichever abundance covariate is used (+16.8 and +7.8 with mean
expression). STATE does so too (+5.5, +9.8 and +4.0). But when the
gene's mean expression is the covariate, STATE's nuclear/surface
organisation disappears (+0.2). Its other two axes remain (+7.0 and
+3.3). STATE and scGPT receive each gene's expression level as input;
MaxToki receives only the genes' rank order. scGPT organises the change
only weakly (+1.4, +2.3 and +3.5). (3) In no representation does any
functional axis significantly exceed gene sets matched on size and
co-expression (smallest p = 0.07, 0.19, 0.23 and 0.32), or modules of
strongly co-expressed genes. (4) No model shows context-specific gene
function. Directional congruence is not significant after correction in
any of the four models, and in none does a TF's closeness to its curated
targets rise in the cell types where those targets are active.
Expression alone can pass, or nearly pass, each test. In the centroid
version we use, the closeness rises with target activity (mean ρ =
+0.214; 9 of 11 TFs positive; p = 0.03, not corrected; 0.10 after
Bonferroni over the three curated-target questions). Its build with all
expressing cells, which we report only as a check, passes the
directional test on the transcription/transport axis (β = +0.11, p =
0.007, significant after Holm correction over 3 tests), where its
matched null is narrow (standard deviation 0.05, against 0.11--0.16 in
MaxToki-217M); the build we use does not (p = 0.113). So a pass on
either test would not by itself show gene function beyond expression.
One static signal appears: STATE places TFs closer to their curated
targets than to co-expression-matched non-targets (24 TFs, mean cosine
excess +0.099, p = 7.7 × 10\textsuperscript{−4}). Compared with the
targets of the other TFs tested, a STATE TF is slightly closer to its
own targets (+0.047; 17 of 24 TFs positive; p = 0.032, not corrected;
0.096 after Bonferroni over the three curated-target questions). So we
cannot say that the closeness is specific to each TF's own targets. The
untrained STATE, which uses the same fixed protein-embedding gene table
as the trained model, shows the same closeness (+0.132, p = 1.4 ×
10\textsuperscript{−4}) and is about as specific to each TF's own
targets (+0.040; 17 of 24 TFs positive; p = 0.032). Untrained scGPT and
MaxToki-217M show no significant closeness (−0.008, p = 0.895; +0.030,
with 6 of 11 TFs positive, p = 0.500; this 11-TF test has little power).
So STATE's closeness does not come from training on expression. It most
likely comes from the protein-sequence embeddings; we did not run STATE
with a random gene table, which would test this directly. scGPT shows a
different pattern. Its TFs are not closer to their targets than to
co-expression-matched non-targets (+0.026; p = 0.402). But they are
closer to their own targets than to the targets of the other TFs tested
(+0.070; 13 of 16 TFs positive; p = 0.011, not corrected; p = 0.011 at
layer 2), and untrained scGPT is not (−0.005; p = 0.962). This
comparison is not matched on co-expression, so the scGPT result most
likely reflects co-expression between a TF and its targets, learned in
training, rather than a signal beyond co-expression. (5) Only
MaxToki-217M passes the tightness control. It clearly exceeds gene sets
matched on size and representation tightness on two of its three axes (p
= 1/301 for nuclear/surface and 1/301 for transcription/transport;
mitochondrion/cytoskeleton p = 0.053). MaxToki-1B reaches p = 0.033 on
one axis (transcription/transport); this p is not corrected and does not
survive a Holm correction over the model's three axes (0.10). scGPT and
STATE exceed the null on no axis (Table 3). So only in MaxToki-217M is
the organisation clearly not a by-product of compact gene sets.

\subsection{Contextualisation across architectures and sizes, and with
untrained
models}\label{contextualisation-across-architectures-and-sizes-and-with-untrained-models}

Functional organisation can be measured whether or not a model shows
context-specific gene function, so it could serve as a way to compare
models (Table 5, Fig. 5). Here every representation shares the cells,
cell types, partitions and gene panel. (1) Every model contextualises
genes strongly (EXCESS +0.740, +0.767, +0.813 and +0.867 for
MaxToki-217M, MaxToki-1B, scGPT and STATE at depth-matched layers; these
differ slightly from Table 3 because more cell-type pairs are scored
here, Methods), but contextualisation does not grow consistently with
model size. At matched relative depth, MaxToki-1B gives +0.767 (layer 7)
against +0.740 for MaxToki-217M (layer 4), and +0.766 (layer 4) against
+0.832 (layer 2). Functional organisation is not consistently higher in
the larger model either: functional-z is +7.2 against +15.5 at relative
depth 0.35, and +18.0 against +18.4 at 0.2. The depth pattern also
differs between models. From the earlier to the later layer, EXCESS
falls in MaxToki-217M (+0.829 to +0.738) and slightly in STATE (+0.879,
+0.867 and +0.842 at layers 3, 6 and 12), stays the same in MaxToki-1B
(+0.764 and +0.764) and rises in scGPT (+0.687, +0.813 and +0.872 at
layers 2, 4 and 9; all depth values in this sentence use cell-type pairs
with at least 200 count-balanced genes, as in Table 3). (2) On the
nuclear/surface axis and each model's own entries, functional
organisation is highest in the MaxToki models and much lower in scGPT
and STATE (+3.7 and +4.0). The order between the other models depends on
the gene set and the controls. On the entries shared by all
representations, STATE (+6.8) is above scGPT (+3.3) and MaxToki-1B at
layer 7 (+4.8). After the rank regression, STATE is above both MaxToki
models on the mitochondrion/cytoskeleton axis (+9.8 against +7.5 and
+8.1; Table 3), but with the gene's mean expression as the covariate it
is below both (+7.0 against +9.1 and +8.8). On the nuclear/surface axis,
only MaxToki-217M stays clearly above scGPT and STATE in every version:
each model's own entries, the shared entries, the 9-cell-type cut and
both abundance covariates. On the other two axes it is not clearly above
STATE. The models differ in more than input (training data, objective
and gene vocabulary), so this comparison does not isolate the cause, and
the functional organisation is not yet a stable way to rank
architectures. (3) Part of the contextualisation needs no training in
every architecture. Untrained versions keep 60\% (MaxToki-217M), 36\%
(scGPT) and 88\% (STATE) of the trained EXCESS. Untrained MaxToki-217M
keeps a much smaller share of the functional organisation (functional-z
+4.6 against +15.5; +4.1 against +11.3 on the shared entries). In scGPT
and STATE the trained values are already low (+3.7 and +4.0), so these
comparisons tell us little about whether training adds organisation.
Untrained scGPT gives +2.2. Untrained STATE gives +1.4 on its own
entries, but +3.8 against +6.8 on the shared entries. After the rank
regression, untrained STATE keeps about half of the trained organisation
on the nuclear/surface and mitochondrion/cytoskeleton axes (+3.0 against
+5.5; +4.8 against +9.8) and is above the trained model on
transcription/transport (+5.6 against +4.0). So in STATE much of the
functional organisation does not need training. In untrained scGPT the
functional axes barely separate their two poles (validity AUC
0.51--0.57), so its values cannot be read. We did not test why the
untrained STATE keeps most of the contextualisation. Two things could
contribute: each gene's input token carries its expression level, which
itself differs between cell types, and the untrained model uses the same
fixed protein-embedding gene table as the trained one. Running STATE
with random weights and a random gene table would separate the two; we
did not run it. The expression-only representations show little or no
functional organisation (−1.3, +0.7 and −0.4). Their EXCESS is lower
(+0.424, +0.218 and +0.320), but it depends on how many cells each
vector averages (+0.607 for the centroid version with all expressing

models from expression. On the 14,109 shared entries EXCESS changes

MaxToki-1B is above MaxToki-217M at relative depth 0.2 (+15.1 against
+13.5), and untrained STATE is above trained scGPT (+3.8 against +3.3).

\subsection{Contextualisation is weakly correlated with what the model
gains from context in
prediction}\label{contextualisation-is-weakly-correlated-with-what-the-model-gains-from-context-in-prediction}

If contextualisation serves the autoregressive objective, genes the
model contextualises more should gain more from real context in
next-gene prediction. We find a weak positive link of this kind. Real
context helps prediction a great deal: across 2266 comparisons for 558
genes, the mean benefit is +12.0 nats (median +12.9; a nat is a
natural-log unit of probability), so the true gene is roughly
160,000-fold more probable under its real context than under another
cell's. The per-gene size of this benefit is weakly related to how much
the gene moves between cell types: the partial Spearman correlation,
controlling for how often the gene was sampled as a target, is +0.161
(95\% CI +0.083 to +0.239). Genes whose representations depend more on
context tend, weakly, to be genes whose prediction depends more on
context.

The link holds in MaxToki-1B and beyond cell identity. In the paired
design (the same 350 immune cells, positions and donors for both models,
with each gene's movement taken from the 600-cell extractions), the
partial correlation is +0.133 (95\% CI +0.040 to +0.222) for
MaxToki-217M and +0.200 (+0.108 to +0.280) for MaxToki-1B with a random
donor. When the donor comes from the same cell type, real context still
helps a great deal (mean benefit +10.83 and +10.36 nats, against +12.07
and +11.53 with a random donor), and the correlation stays positive
(+0.236, CI +0.147 to +0.322; +0.323, CI +0.242 to +0.400). It is higher
than with a random donor in both models, but we did not test this
difference, and the two arms use slightly different genes. So most of
the benefit of real context is specific to the cell, not to its cell
type, and it is somewhat larger for the genes the model contextualises
more. These tests used immune cells only.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The results characterise how SCFMs represent genes in context and what
that representation carries. All four models change a gene's
representation with cell type in a gene-specific and reproducible way.
In MaxToki-217M, the only model we measured at every layer, this change
is most reproducible in the first one or two layers, while genes keep
moving between cell types in later layers. This depth pattern is not
shared by every model. The other models were measured at only two or
three layers: in scGPT, EXCESS rises with depth (+0.687, +0.813 and
+0.872 at layers 2, 4 and 9), in STATE it falls slightly (+0.879, +0.867
and +0.842 at layers 3, 6 and 12), and in MaxToki-1B it is the same at
layers 4 and 7 (+0.764). Untrained versions of the three architectures
keep part of the change (36--88\% of the trained EXCESS), so some
contextualisation comes from the architecture and the input alone.
Training strengthens it. In MaxToki-217M it also strengthens its
alignment with functional axes (functional-z +4.6 before training and
+15.5 after); in STATE much of that alignment is already there before
training. In both MaxToki sizes the change is weakly correlated, gene by
gene, with how much the model's next-gene predictions in immune cells
depend on the cell's own context, beyond its cell type. To our knowledge
this is the first measurement for single-cell models of the ``how
contextual are contextualised representations'' question (Ethayarajh
2019). On the nuclear/surface axis, the functional organisation of the
change separates MaxToki-217M from scGPT and STATE in every version of
the analysis (functional-z +15.5 against +3.7 and +4.0; +16.7 against
+1.4 and +5.5 after the rank regression). On the other two axes it does
not: on the mitochondrion/cytoskeleton axis STATE is higher after the
rank regression (+9.8 against +7.5) but lower with mean expression as
the covariate (+7.0 against +9.1), and on the transcription/transport
axis the three models are close (+5.0, +3.5 and +4.0). The measure also
does not order the other models consistently, so it is not yet a stable
way to rank architectures.

What the change carries is organised by co-expression. In no model is
the functional organisation significantly stronger than that of gene
sets matched to the functional sets on size and average co-expression
(smallest p = 0.07), and modules of strongly co-expressed genes produce
more. This test can detect only large differences, so a small functional
effect beyond co-expression cannot be ruled out. Expression alone gives
part of this picture but not all of it. A representation that places
each gene where it is expressed within each cell type also changes in a
gene-specific way (EXCESS +0.321 when each vector averages 50 cells, as
in the models, and +0.605 with all expressing cells), so a reproducible,
gene-specific change is not by itself a sign of learning. EXCESS values
of representations with different widths, inputs and sample sizes should
not be ranked against each other. But in none of the three
expression-only versions is the change organised along functional axes
or along co-expressed gene sets: strong co-expression modules reach
1.4--1.5, 1.5 and 0.9--1.0 times the power of random gene sets, against
5.6--10.1 times in MaxToki-217M, 5.0--8.9 in MaxToki-1B, 3.3--4.2 in
scGPT and 12.5--18.0 in STATE. So the models move genes along directions
that separate co-expressed gene sets much more than these
expression-only versions do, for co-expressed sets in general rather
than for functional sets in particular. We do not claim that the models
add information beyond co-expression; other expression-only
constructions, in particular ones that see co-expression between cell
types, might come closer. The two direct tests for context-specific gene
function are negative in every model. An expression-only representation
can pass the directional test (the centroid build with all expressing
cells) and nearly pass the target-activity test (the centroid build we
use; p = 0.03 before correction), so a pass would not by itself show
function beyond expression. The one signal beyond co-expression is
static: in STATE, and more weakly in the larger MaxToki-217M extraction,
TFs sit closer to their curated targets than to co-expression-matched
non-targets, but not more so in the cell types where those targets are
active. Compared with the targets of other TFs, the closeness is not
clearly specific to each TF's own targets in either model (STATE: 17 of
24 TFs positive, p = 0.032 before correction; MaxToki-217M: 17 of 26
TFs, p = 0.084), so it may partly reflect where regulators and their
usual targets sit in general. scGPT TFs are closer to their own targets
than to other TFs' targets, but not closer than co-expression-matched
non-targets, so that result is consistent with co-expression alone. In
STATE this closeness is already present before training and, if
anything, larger; untrained models without a fixed gene table show none.
So it most likely comes from the protein-sequence embeddings that serve
as STATE's gene tokens, not from learning on expression data; we did not
run STATE with a random gene table, which would test this directly. For
MaxToki-217M we cannot say whether the closeness is learned: we did not
run an untrained model on the 1000-cell extraction, and on the 600-cell
extraction neither the trained nor the untrained model shows it.

The steering results do not test co-expression. Both MaxToki sizes read
the nuclear/surface direction more than directions built in the same way
from random splits of the same genes. In MaxToki-217M part of this
effect goes through its two largest hidden dimensions: with them set to
zero, the effect is no longer clearly above the splits (p = 0.065).
MaxToki-1B also reads the other two functional directions. The random
splits mix the two poles, so they are not matched to them on
co-expression, and a model that reads co-expressed gene sets would also
pass this test. The pushes are about 1.4 to 6 times a gene's typical
(median) natural range along the axis, and each moves half of a cell's
genes at once, so the tests show that the models can read these
directions, not that natural context changes along them are used.
MaxToki-217M reads the transcription/transport direction in reverse, and
its mitochondrion/cytoskeleton direction is not distinct from random
splits. The architecture comparison does not contradict the main
conclusion. On the nuclear/surface axis STATE organises the change more
weakly than MaxToki-217M in every version we ran, and its organisation
there disappears when the gene's mean expression is the covariate (+0.2,
against +5.5 with the rank covariate). Against MaxToki-1B the order
depends on the gene set: on each model's own entries STATE is lower
(+5.5 against +8.0 after the rank regression), and on the entries shared
by all representations it is higher (+6.8 against +4.8). On the other
two axes STATE is about as strong as the MaxToki models. scGPT organises
the change only weakly. In all four models the organisation stays within
the co-expression nulls. The comparison cannot separate input type from
the other differences between the models.

One possible reason why we found no context-specific gene function
beyond co-expression is that these models are trained only on expression
data. They see which genes are expressed and either their rank order
(MaxToki) or their expression level (scGPT and STATE). STATE also gets a
fixed protein-sequence embedding for each gene. Their training rewards
predicting co-expressed genes. The link between contextualisation and
the benefit of real context is consistent with that reading, although
the test measures only how far a gene moves, not in which direction.
Some context-dependent function acts at the protein level, as in
moonlighting proteins that switch function with location or binding
partners (Jeffery 1999), and need not change expression. Other forms,
such as cell-type-specific enhancer choice by lineage-determining TFs
(Heinz et al. 2010), act through target-gene expression and could in
principle be visible; our tests did not detect them. STATE's
protein-sequence gene tokens are the same in every cell type, so they
cannot carry a gene's changed role by themselves. We did not test
multi-omic models, models that read regulatory DNA sequence together
with expression, or much larger expression models.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{limitations}

The depth of analysis differs between models. The rank control, the
co-expression and tightness nulls, both Level 2 tests and the
expression-only comparison were run on the four trained models and three
expression-only representations, on the same 600 cells per cell type.
The untrained models went through EXCESS, functional-z against random
axes and the curated-target test; untrained scGPT and STATE also went
through the rank regression on the functional axes. Each untrained model
was built once from one random seed, and we built an untrained
MaxToki-217M only, not an untrained MaxToki-1B. The Setty replication,
the full depth profile and the 1000-cell analyses were run on
MaxToki-217M only; on the Setty data we computed only EXCESS and
functional-z against random axes. Steering and the prediction link were
run on the two MaxToki models only, and the cells that were pushed or
scored were immune cells (30 for steering, 350 for the prediction link).
We did not steer scGPT or STATE: neither has a next-gene readout
comparable to MaxToki's, and their expression heads would first need a
check that they track the input, which we did not do. With 600 cells per
cell type fewer genes reach the occurrence cap in many cell types, and
only 11 TFs qualified for the curated-target test in the MaxToki models
and the expression-only representations (16 for scGPT, 24 for STATE), so
that test has little power for those representations. The Level 2 tests
were run only with genes count-balanced in at least 6 cell types. Each
model has its own count-balanced entries; on the entries shared by all
representations EXCESS barely changes, but the functional-z order
changes for STATE, scGPT and MaxToki-1B, and only EXCESS and
nuclear/surface functional-z were repeated on the shared entries.
Functional-z also moves with the draw of random axes, so only clear
differences between models should be read as real. EXCESS values should
not be ranked across architectures, because widths and input types
differ. Input lengths also differ: MaxToki saw each cell's top 1022
genes by count divided by gene median, scGPT a random 1199 of its
expressed genes and STATE the top 2047 by count. scGPT and STATE receive
each gene's expression level; the rank regression used MaxToki's mean
rank on the same cells. Repeating it with mean log-normalised expression
left every co-expression-null result and MaxToki's organisation
unchanged but removed STATE's nuclear/surface organisation (Additional
file 1); we did not repeat it for the EXCESS rank regression, the
tightness null or the two Level 2 tests.

The matched co-expression null is wide: depending on the model, an axis
would need about 1.5 to 2.9 times the matched-set mean to reach p
\textless{} 0.05, so this test could detect only a large excess. It also
matches only average pairwise co-expression; power depends on how
co-expression is distributed within a gene set. Two of the three
expression-only representations use correlations within each cell type
and barely respond to co-expression. The third is built on axes from all
cell types pooled, but each vector is still an average over cells of one
cell type, so it may not capture co-expression between cell types
either; none of the three responds to co-expressed gene sets. So the
contrast between the models and these versions does not show that the
models add anything beyond co-expression in the data. The third
version's result also depends on how it is built: with 512 instead of 50
principal components it failed the positive control, and we did not run
other numbers of components through the pipeline. Other constructions,
such as gene embeddings learned from the same cells or a co-expression
network embedding, were not tested and might reproduce more of the
organisation. The rank control rests on regressions and a rank-matched
comparison; it cannot exclude every way in which position could enter a
causal model. Most analyses use one layer per model, at matched relative
depth, not the peak layers 1--2 of MaxToki-217M; the other models were
not measured at every layer. The steering direction is the
context-averaged difference between two gene classes, not the
context-specific change itself, and the pushes are large; we did not
steer along directions built from gene sets matched to the poles on
co-expression, so steering cannot separate function from co-expression;
and with 30 random splits, the split-control p values cannot survive a
correction for several tests. The prediction link is a correlation
across genes, and it controls only for how often a gene was sampled as a
target. A gene whose expression varies more from cell to cell could both
move more between cell types and gain more from its own context,
whatever the model does with that movement. We did not control for this,
for example with the gene's rank change or the spread of its expression,
and we did not check whether movement measured in the untrained models
or the expression-only representations shows the same link. The Setty
data cover a pre-filtered set of genes, unlike the full raw counts of
Tabula Sapiens. Cell type is partly tied to tissue and donor. Of the 12
cell types, 5 come from one dataset only: 4 from lung, and kidney
epithelial cells, all from a single donor. Most pulmonary alveolar type
1 cells (915 of 1000) also come from one donor. Assay mix differs less:
in every cell type at least 86\% of cells are 10x 3' v3. The two halves
of a cell type share its tissue, donor and ambient-RNA background, so
differences in these between cell types count toward EXCESS and repeat
in both halves. We did not measure how large this part is, for example
by comparing cell types from the same tissue and donor. In the Setty
data all contexts come from one bone-marrow dataset and EXCESS is
similar (+0.713), so the effect does not need these differences, but
their share in the Tabula Sapiens numbers is unknown. The Level 2
results are negative, but they do not show that context-specific gene
function does not exist. Some of it leaves no trace in expression, and
the directional test could detect only clear congruence, so weaker
effects could be missed. Functional axes are built from direct
annotations in one annotation file; the conclusions hold across three
axes, but specific z-scores depend on how the poles are defined.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

The four models we tested build contextual gene representations that are
worth studying in their own right. A gene's vector changes with its cell
type in a gene-specific, reproducible way. Part of this change comes
from the architecture and part from training, and a representation built
from expression alone also changes in a gene-specific way. In
MaxToki-217M, the only model scanned layer by layer, it is most
reproducible in the first one or two layers; scGPT, measured at three
layers, shows the opposite trend. In both MaxToki sizes, genes that
change more are, weakly, the genes whose next-gene prediction gains more
from the cell's own context. How the change is organised differs between
architectures. The organisation follows co-expression: in four models,
context does not move genes significantly more along functional axes
than along axes built from gene sets with the same co-expression.
Context moves genes along axes built from co-expressed gene sets much
more in the models than in three expression-only representations of the
same cells. Among the things we measured, this is what separates the
models from expression alone. Two direct tests for context-specific gene
function are negative in every model. So we could not read a gene's
changed role in a new cell type from these models, which is the use that
would make them a tool for new hypotheses. Our tests could detect only
clear effects, so this does not prove the models lack it, and whether
models trained on more than expression can show it was not tested.
